PROMPT DESIGN
Methods section draft for the research proposal

1. Overview

The study compares four prompts of increasing structure, each given to a
general-purpose AI assistant together with the same two attachments: one
casing diagram from the random sample and the verbatim text of California
Code of Regulations, title 14, section 1724.7.1 (casing-diagram-specification.txt).
Each prompt represents a level of prompting skill that a worker might bring to
the task described in the business scenario (scenario-business-need.txt): a
regulatory or engineering staff member who wants to know whether an existing
casing diagram meets the State's casing diagram requirements before reusing
it in a Notice of Intention to Abandon.

Each level after the first adds one established prompting technique, cited
once at the level where it is introduced: a structured request built from
persona, task, context, and format (level 2), worked examples (level 3), and
an instruction to reason step by step (level 4).

    Level  Name                              Technique added
    1      Unstructured zero-shot (naive)    none; the request as a worker would type it
    2      Structured zero-shot (PTCF)       persona, task, context, format
    3      Few-shot                          level 2 plus worked examples
    4      Chain-of-thought                  level 3 plus step-by-step reasoning

"Zero-shot" denotes a prompt that gives the model no worked examples;
"few-shot" denotes one that does.


2. Elements held constant across levels

To attribute differences in the responses to the prompt alone, the following
are identical for every run:

  - The two attachments. The diagram is the original file downloaded from
    WellSTAR, unaltered and verified by SHA-256 checksum; the specification
    is the same verbatim text, verified word for word against its source.
  - One prompt, sent once, in a new conversation with no prior history. The
    first response is the data; no follow-up, rephrasing, or regeneration.
  - The AI service and account type within a comparison, with the date, the
    model where displayed, the use of web search, and any upload messages
    recorded for every run.

Each level's prompt is stored in two forms: a copy-and-paste file containing
exactly the text sent, and a documented version recording its construction and
change history.


3. Level 1: Unstructured zero-shot (naive)

Purpose. Level 1 is the baseline: the request a worker without prompting
training would type.

Construction. The prompt was written from the scenario by asking what a worker
at a desk would want to know. Three questions were judged most natural: whether
the diagram is good enough to file, what is missing, and what must be fixed
before submission. An item-by-item review was judged characteristic of an
auditor rather than a worker and was excluded from this level. Four candidate
wordings were compared for realism and for how their responses could be
scored. The selected prompt asks for a verdict and for the gaps, names the
purpose, and uses industry shorthand ("WBD," "notice to abandon"):

    I'm going to use this WBD in our notice to abandon. Does it meet CalGEM's
    requirements, and what's missing?

An earlier draft that asked the AI to "tell me what passes and what fails" was
rejected because "passes and fails" is the study's vocabulary rather than the
worker's.

What is deliberately absent: a persona, a list or definition of the
requirements to check, rating categories, an evidence requirement, a
restriction to the attached files, and any output format.


4. Level 2: Structured zero-shot (Persona-Task-Context-Format)

Purpose. Level 2 represents a worker who structures the request: stating who
the AI should act as, what to do, why, and in what form to answer.

Construction. The prompt was built by writing one sentence or pair of sentences
for each of the four elements of a prompt in Google's guide: persona, task,
context, and format (Google, 2024).

    Persona  You are a regulatory engineer who reviews wellbore diagrams for
             CalGEM filings.
    Task     Check whether the attached WBD meets each requirement in the
             attached CalGEM specification.
    Context  I'm preparing a notice to abandon for this well and want to reuse
             this diagram. The specification is 14 CCR 1724.7.1, word for word.
    Format   Give me a table with each requirement, whether the diagram meets
             it, and what's missing. Keep it short.

The element labels are not sent to the AI; the four parts are sent as one
paragraph, as a worker would type them. A more restrictive draft containing
rating definitions, an evidence requirement, a restriction to the attachments,
and a prohibition on recommendations was set aside as representing a more
advanced level of skill than this step.


5. Level 3: Few-shot

Purpose. Level 3 adds worked examples to the request, the defining feature of
few-shot prompting (Brown et al., 2020).

Construction. The level 2 prompt is kept unchanged and five worked examples are
appended. They are written as a person would type them, in short sentences of
the form "If a diagram shows ..., then (requirement) does not meet," introduced
with "Here's how I want each requirement judged. These are made-up examples,
not from the attached diagram." A first draft presented the examples as a
table; it was rewritten because a worker typing a request does not express
expectations in tables.

The examples are derived from the wording of the regulation, not from any
diagram. Each describes a made-up diagram with invented values and applies one
requirement's wording to it:

    (a)(1)     a requirement listing several items ("Operator name, lease name,
               well number, and API number") is met only when every item is
               shown;
    (a)(2)     a single required item that is shown is met;
    (a)(12)    showing part of a requirement (the top of a cement plug) without
               the rest (its bottom) is not met;
    (d)        "measured depth and true vertical depth" requires both;
    (a)(15)    a clause about the injection project ("the injection zone and
               confining layer(s) for the underground injection project(s)")
               does not apply to a well outside such a project.

Because no example is taken from a real diagram, the examples cannot reveal
answers for the sampled diagrams, nor favor diagrams drawn on a particular
operator's template. The judgments shown, Meets, Does not meet, and Not
applicable, follow from the regulation's own structure: data the diagram "shall
include all of the following data," and provisions limited to certain wells
(the injection project clause of (a)(15), and (c)). An earlier draft that took example rows from a real diagram outside
the sample was rejected for these reasons.


6. Level 4: Chain-of-thought

Purpose. Level 4 asks the model to reason through the task step by step before
giving its answer.

Construction. The level 3 prompt is kept unchanged and one paragraph is added:

    Before you give me the table, work through it step by step. Take the
    requirements one at a time, in order. For each one, find where the diagram
    shows that information, say what you found, and then decide if it meets.
    When you've gone through all of them, give me the table.

Kojima et al. (2022) showed that an instruction to reason step by step,
without worked reasoning examples, produces step-by-step reasoning. They
describe the method as adding "Let's think step by step, or a similar text"
(p. 3), and their comparison of sixteen wordings (Table 4, p. 8) includes
"Let's solve this problem by splitting it into steps." The paragraph used here
is of that kind: it keeps "step by step" and states, in a worker's words, how
the task splits into steps (one requirement at a time, find the information on
the diagram, say what was found, then decide), with the reasoning placed before
the answer. The level 2 request "Keep it short" is left unchanged; how the AI
handles a request for both a step-by-step check and a short answer is
recorded.

A first version added only the sentence "Before you give me the table, think it
through step by step" to the level 2 prompt. It was replaced because current
chat models already reason before answering, so the sentence changed little;
because it did not say what the steps were; and because, built on level 2, it
dropped the examples added at level 3.


7. Why each level builds on the one before

The levels are cumulative: each prompt contains the previous level's prompt
word for word and adds one technique. Level 2 adds structure to level 1's
request, level 3 adds examples to level 2, and level 4 adds step-by-step
reasoning to level 3. Each comparison between adjacent levels therefore
isolates one technique: level 2 against level 1 shows the effect of structure,
level 3 against level 2 the effect of examples, and level 4 against level 3 the
effect of reasoning instructions. The cumulative design also matches the
scenario: each level represents a worker with one more prompting skill than
the last, and a more skilled worker keeps the techniques already learned. That
each level begins with the previous level's text is checked automatically for
every prompt file.


8. How the responses will be evaluated

Accuracy. Each response is scored against reference answers prepared by the
researcher for the same diagram, one judgment per requirement of section
1724.7.1, coded Present, Absent, or Illegible. Each requirement in the response
is classed as correctly judged, wrongly judged, or not addressed. A requirement
the response does not mention is not counted as met.

Cost to the worker. The dry run of level 1 produced a long response in which a
large share addressed matters other than the task: advice on preparing a
proposed abandonment diagram, a discussion of whether the regulation applied,
citations to sources outside the attachments, and a closing question to the
user. A worker must read and sort all of it to reach the answer. Each response
is therefore also measured for length (words), the share of the response that
addresses the task, the use of sources outside the attachments, and whether it
ends with a question back to the user.

Input quality. Results are reported for all diagrams and separately by input
type (Native, Mixed, Scanned), as set out in ../../sample/sample-quality-evaluation.txt.


9. Limitations

- The free tier of the AI service used for the dry run does not display the
  model that produced a response, and the provider may change that model at
  any time. Runs for a comparison should be made within a short period.
- Kojima et al. (2022, Table 4) show that the wording of the step-by-step
  instruction affects accuracy, so the worker's phrasing used at level 4 may
  not perform exactly as "Let's think step by step."
- Prompts are written in the researcher's judgment of how a worker would write
  them; they have not been collected from workers.
