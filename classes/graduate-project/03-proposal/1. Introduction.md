# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Background and Context

Technology is both a product and a medium of human action (Orlikowski, 1992). In 2026, this statement is especially relevant to artificial intelligence (AI), a technology that people now build, shape, and work through every day. From east to west and from north to south, AI has reached nearly every country: ChatGPT traffic was recorded in 209 of 218 economies within 16 months of its release, although use remains far heavier in wealthier countries (Liu & Wang, 2026). People do not act alone, however. Much of their action takes place inside organizations, and those organizations are being affected as well. Individuals have embraced AI faster than the firms that employ them, and most firms are still working out how it can add value to their business (Liu & Wang, 2026). Interest and curiosity are widespread, but many people and organizations may not yet know with precision whether, or how, AI fits their for-profit activities.

That uncertainty weighs on two groups. The first is the established company that operated successfully for years or decades without AI and now feels compelled to adopt it, often before the return on that investment is clear. The second is the worker who built a career inside such a company, already handles many tasks, and now meets AI as an outside agent in work that was already done well. Neither group needs another general claim that AI is powerful. Both need evidence of how well it performs a task they already know.

The oil and gas industry offers such a task. In California, the California Geologic Energy Management Division (CalGEM) requires operators to submit casing diagrams, drawings of how each well is built, and Section 1724.7.1 of Title 14 of the California Code of Regulations states that the diagram "shall include" 15 data items (Casing Diagrams, 2026a, subd. (a)). Checking whether a diagram contains every required item is routine work for the people who prepare and review well records, and its outcome can be judged against the regulation.

## 1.2 Problem Statement

Incomplete casing diagrams carry regulatory consequences. In 2025, CalGEM ordered an operator to correct the casing diagrams for two injection projects and to pay a civil penalty because the diagrams lacked required items, including the spud date and true vertical depth at all depths (CalGEM, 2025b). In the same year, CalGEM told underground gas storage operators that it had "observed inconsistencies in the submission of updated casing diagrams" (CalGEM, 2025a, p. 1). Completeness checks of this kind are performed by people, one diagram at a time.

Free AI assistants such as ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini can read an uploaded drawing and compare it with a list, so a company might use them for this check without building or buying a specialized system. Whether they do so reliably is not known. Large language models can assess documents against required-item checklists, but with performance that varies by model and checklist (Kataoka et al., 2026; Srinivasan et al., 2025), and general-purpose models have shown low accuracy on some engineering-drawing tasks (Doris et al., 2025; Picard et al., 2025). Within the sources and searches reviewed for this proposal (see Section 2), no study has tested free consumer assistants on the completeness of engineering drawings against a legal checklist. Prompt wording also changes results, sometimes for the better and sometimes for the worse (Kojima et al., 2022; Sorin et al., 2025), so a company does not know which way of asking produces a trustworthy answer.

## 1.3 Purpose and Research Questions

The purpose of this study is to measure, within the frame of academic research, how well free consumer AI assistants perform a familiar and common task: determining whether a California casing diagram is complete against CalGEM's requirements. ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini will each be given 30 casing diagrams under three prompt types, naive, informed, and chain-of-thought, for 270 runs. Each response will be scored item by item against a gold standard prepared by the researcher. The study asks the following questions:

- **RQ1.** How accurately do free consumer AI assistants judge the presence or absence of the 15 items required by Section 1724.7.1(a) in California casing diagrams?
- **RQ2.** Does the accuracy of those judgments differ across the three prompt types?
- **RQ3.** Does accuracy increase in order from the naive prompt to the informed prompt to the chain-of-thought prompt?
- **RQ4.** Which adjacent step adds accuracy: adding the informing elements (informed compared with naive) or adding step-by-step reasoning (chain-of-thought compared with informed)?

## 1.4 Significance of the Study

For established companies, the study offers evidence on one concrete use of AI before money and work are committed to it. It does not measure financial return. It shows how often free tools reach the same judgment as a careful human reviewer on a task the company already performs and already knows how to judge, and which way of asking works best. That evidence can inform a decision to adopt, limit, or postpone AI for similar compliance checks.

For workers, the study treats their existing expertise as the standard. The human review is the gold standard, and the AI is measured against it. The results show where the assistant agrees with that judgment, where it errs, and where it says nothing, which is the information a worker needs to decide whether to use the tool, how far to trust it, and where to keep checking by hand.

For research, the study extends checklist-assessment studies from scientific text to scanned engineering drawings, applies the data-quality concept of completeness (Lee et al., 2006) to a regulatory document, and tests consumer tools as companies and workers can actually obtain them. For regulators such as CalGEM, it indicates whether widely available tools could help operators find missing items before diagrams are submitted.

## 1.5 Definitions of Key Terms

**Casing diagram.** A drawing of a well's construction that, under Section 1724.7.1(a), must include 15 data items (Casing Diagrams, 2026a).

**Completeness.** The degree to which required values are present. In this study, a casing diagram is complete to the degree that the required items that apply to the well are shown (Lee et al., 2006, p. 56; see Section 3.2.1).

**Consumer AI assistant.** A publicly available chat interface to a large language model that can be used without a subscription. This study uses the free versions of ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini.

**Prompt type.** The way the task is asked. A *naive* prompt states the task only. An *informed* prompt adds a persona, task, context, and format. A *chain-of-thought* prompt adds an instruction to reason step by step.

**Gold standard.** The researcher's answer key, which records whether each required item is shown, missing, or not applicable for each diagram. It is used only for scoring and is never given to the AI model.

**Run.** One prompt type applied to one casing diagram on one AI model, producing one response.

**Run score.** The number of the 15 items, from 0 to 15, that the model judged correctly in one run.

**WellSTAR.** CalGEM's public online database of well records, the source of the casing diagrams (CalGEM, n.d.-b).
