 a b s t r a c t
The rapid adoption of large language models (LLMs) and generative artiﬁcial intelligence (AI) systems has 
brought AI hallucinations to the forefront of contemporary natural language processing research. AI hallucina-
tions refer to statements made by the system which are either factually incorrect, illogical, or not substantiated by 
any credible sources but sound coherent and ﬂuent on the face of it. These behaviors raise serious problems when 
using AI in high-stakes applications such as medicine, teaching, legal matters, ﬁnance, or science, where accurate 
facts are required. The present survey aims to provide an extensive overview of current techniques employed 
to detect, verify, mitigate, or correct hallucinations in generative AI models. Initially, we provide a taxonomy 
of diﬀerent types of AI hallucinations. Subsequently, we examine major detection and veriﬁcation paradigms, 
including retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), factuality evaluation metrics, automated fact-checking, natu-
ral language inference (NLI), knowledge-grounded reasoning, multi-model veriﬁcation, and self-reﬂection-based 
approaches. The survey further reviews widely used benchmark datasets, evaluation frameworks, and emerging 
multi-layer veriﬁcation architectures for improving AI trustworthiness. In addition, enabling technologies such 
as transformer-based language models, dense retrieval systems, knowledge graphs, credibility assessment mech-
anisms, and truth-scoring frameworks are analyzed from the perspective of reliable content veriﬁcation. Lastly, 
we highlight important limitations of existing approaches along with potential future research directions, such 
as scaling veriﬁcation, multilingual reasoning, real-time veriﬁcation, and privacy-enhanced trusted artiﬁcial in-
telligence systems. In summary, this survey oﬀers a comprehensive guide to researchers and developers aiming 
to design reliable, interpretable, and trustworthy generative AI models.

1.1.  Motivation and scope
The rapid proliferation of large language models across high-stakes 
domains including healthcare, law, education, and scientiﬁc research 
has exposed a critical and persistent reliability gap: while individual 
veriﬁcation techniques such as retrieval-augmented generation (Lewis 
et al., 2020), factuality scoring (Min et al., 2023b), and cross-model val-
idation (Adlakha et al., 2024) have advanced signiﬁcantly in isolation, 
no uniﬁed framework has consolidated these fragmented advances into 
a single, model-agnostic, end-to-end veriﬁcation pipeline. This gap is not 
merely technical; its consequences are societal. Hallucinated drug inter-
actions in clinical decision support, fabricated legal citations in judicial 
proceedings, and invented statistics in scientiﬁc literature represent doc-
umented failure modes with real-world impact (Beauvais, 2022; Adams 
et al., 2023). As Broda and Strömbäck (Broda and Strömbäck, 2024) 
demonstrate through interdisciplinary analysis, the unchecked diﬀusion 
of AI generated misinformation systematically erodes public trust in sci-
ence, journalism, and institutional authority.
Despite extensive research investment, the veriﬁcation landscape re-
mains fragmented along three dimensions. First, methodological frag-
mentation: RAG systems (Lewis et al., 2020; Shuster et al., 2021) re-
duce but do not eliminate hallucination, as RAGTruth (Niu et al., 2024) 
empirically conﬁrms; factuality metrics such as FActScore (Min et al., 
2023b), QAFactEval (Fabbri et al., 2022), and FEQA (Durmus et al., 
2020) measure consistency without providing interactive correction; 
and cross-model validation (Shami et al., 2026; Adlakha et al., 2024) 
shows promise without integration into deployable pipelines. Second, 
taxonomic fragmentation: Huang et al. (2025a) and Pesaranghader and 
Li (2026) identify four distinct hallucination categories factual, faithful, 
logical, and temporal yet no existing system addresses all four simulta-
neously. Third, operational fragmentation: existing solutions are model-
speciﬁc, non-interpretable, or lack automated correction, limiting their 
applicability in real-world deployments where model-agnosticism, ex-
plainability, and actionability are required (Bommasani et al., 2021; 
Thorne, 2024).
This survey is motivated by the need to resolve this three-
dimensional fragmentation. Synthesizing peer-reviewed literature span-
ning 2016 to 2026 across eight research dimensions hallucination tax-
onomy and evaluation, retrieval-augmented generation, automated fact-
checking, NLP foundations, truth scoring and factuality metrics, mis-
information detection, trustworthy AI frameworks, and cross-model 
veriﬁcation we provide the ﬁrst comprehensive, structured synthesis 
of hallucination mitigation research and propose TruthGuard AI as a 
theoretically-driven concept implementation of the uniﬁed veriﬁcation 
framework, developed by directly analyzing the observed patterns from 
the reviewed literature. The TruthGuard AI is considered as a concep-
tual framework in this literature survey rather than being an empirically 
tested one; a full empirical analysis including datasets, baselines, imple-
mentations, and ablation tests is out of the scope of the current literature 
survey and is noted as an important future direction (Section 9.2). The 
procedure of search and screening that was performed for this literature 
survey and its description, including databases, search strings, screening 
phases, and criteria for inclusion/exclusion, can be found in Section 2.
The scope of this survey is bounded as follows. We focus exclusively 
on text-generating LLMs and their hallucination failure modes; multi-
modal veriﬁcation (Jiang et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2024; Wang et al., 
2024) is discussed as a future extension but not comprehensively sur-
veyed. We cover veriﬁcation approaches applicable as post-generation 
middleware systems that assess and correct LLM outputs without requir-
ing access to model internals consistent with the model-agnostic deploy-
ment principle articulated by Bommasani et al. (2021). This model-
agnostic approach is in reference to model-parameter independence 
speciﬁcally; the limitations of this independence, considering the fact 
that veriﬁcation is dependent on the output features of the generat-
ing model, are explored in Section 7. We restrict our primary empiri-
cal analysis to English-language systems and general-domain content, 
while identifying multilingual and domain-speciﬁc extension as a pri-
ority future direction (Panchendrarajan and Zubiaga, 2026; Panchen-
drarajan et al., 2025). The survey covers literature from 2016, the year 
of the foundational AI safety framework of Amodei et al. (2016), through 
2026, encompassing the most recent advances in LLM-based veriﬁcation 
research.

2.  Review methodology
The transparency and reproducibility of the literature synthesis con-
ducted in this survey require that the systematic process of identiﬁca-
tion, screening, and selection of the studies discussed in Sections 3–9 be 
reported, following the staged process represented in Fig. 1.
2.1.  Preliminary investigation
This is where the systematic literature synthesis presented in this sur-
vey took its start. A preliminary investigation was made ﬁrst in order to 
gain clarity on the scope of the search and focus the scope of the review 
before the systematic screening. The preliminary search was conducted 
using Google Scholar with the key search term phrase “hallucination 
detection generative AI”, as well as two or more additional terms di-
rectly related to it, for example, “factuality”, “retrieval-augmented gen-
eration”, and “fact-checking”.
After ﬁnding an initial set of relevant papers, additional keywords 
were identiﬁed based on their abstracts, and the bibliographic databases 
likely to index the target literature were found. Thereafter, the search 
was extended to ﬁve bibliographic databases – Scopus, Web of Science, 
IEEE Xplore, the ACM Digital Library, and the ACL Anthology – com-
plemented with searches on arXiv and citation chaining using Google 
Scholar for recent preprints not yet indexed elsewhere. Search dates 
were limited to January 2016 – the date of publication of the seminal 
AI safety framework paper by Amodei et al. (2016) – through 30 June 
2026.
Search queries were built using Boolean operators and consisted of 
three concept groups, for example: (“hallucination” OR “factuality” OR 
“factual consistency”) AND (“large language model” OR “LLM” OR “gen-
erative AI” OR “natural language generation”) AND (“detection” OR 
“veriﬁcation” OR “fact-checking” OR “retrieval-augmented generation” 
OR “mitigation” OR “correction”), adapted to the ﬁeld tagging and trun-
cation features of each database.
2.2.  Conducting research
Database search was chosen as the primary search strategy due to the 
sheer number of papers and speed of publication in this domain mak-
ing it impossible to rely solely on manual searching; citation chaining 
using Google Scholar supplemented the structured searches with recent 
preprints not yet indexed elsewhere. The following inclusion and exclu-
sion criteria were used uniformly in screening all the records found in 
the database searches conducted according to Section 2.1.
Inclusion: The records were included if they (i) were peer-reviewed 
journal articles, peer-reviewed conference papers, or oﬃcial  
technical/model reports from major AI laboratories; (ii) were 
written in English; (iii) were directly addressing hallucination 
detection, veriﬁcation, mitigation, or correction in generative AI 
or large language models, or were directly addressing a founda-
tional NLP, retrieval, or evaluation technique that the surveyed 
hallucination literature relied upon; and (iv) were published be-
tween January 2016 and the search cut-oﬀ date.
Exclusion: The records were excluded if they were (i) non-peer-
reviewed blog posts, preprints without a peer-reviewed or oﬃ-
cially released technical report, or opinion pieces; (ii) duplicates 
across databases; (iii) studies where the full text could not be ac-
cessed; or (iv) studies unrelated to text-generating LLMs or to the 
technologies, application domains, and evaluation frameworks 
covered in this survey.
Screening was carried out in two steps: (1) title-and-abstract screen-
ing against the criteria above, followed by (2) full-text screening of 
the screened-out records. Diﬀerences in screening decisions were solved 
through discussion within the author team until reaching agreement.
2.3.  Examining publications
Examining publications for their relevance was a two-step process. 
First, an initial pool of approximately 600 candidate records was identi-
ﬁed in the database and citation chaining searches performed according 
to Section 2.1. Second, the relevant records were examined and selected 
as those signiﬁcant in relation to the research aims: title-and-abstract 
screening against the inclusion and exclusion criteria of Section 2.2 re-
duced the initial pool to approximately 300 records selected for full-
text screening. In relation to their relevance, the record was selected 
in the review only if the topic of hallucination detection, veriﬁcation, 
mitigation, or correction was the central methodological focus of this 
paper, or if the paper addressed a foundational NLP, retrieval, or eval-
uation technique that the surveyed hallucination literature relied upon.

3.  Background and deﬁnitions
This section reviews the foundational concepts that underpin the hal-
lucination veriﬁcation literature. Similar to how the development of 
industrial standards involved agreeing on terminology before the cre-
ation of technology to enable the application (Huang et al., 2025a), the 
AI hallucination community has devoted considerable eﬀort to creating 
precise deﬁnitions. Deﬁnitions for AI hallucinations, fact veriﬁcation, 
trustworthy AI, and reliability veriﬁcation are discussed from various 
viewpoints provided by practitioners in the ﬁeld.
3.1.  AI hallucinations: deﬁnitions and taxonomy
The concept of hallucination with regard to natural language gener-
ation can be deﬁned as the generation of something which has no basis 
or even contradicts what has been provided from knowledge or input 
sources. Various deﬁnitions have been proposed in the literature.
Deﬁnition 1. Huang et al. (2025a) describe hallucination as any gen-
erated text that does not align with real-life facts, contradicts the given 
context, or is self-inconsistent in its generated output. This classiﬁcation 
system classiﬁes hallucinations according to three parameters, namely, 
factuality, faithfulness, and consistency, with the underlying reasons be-
ing training data errors and attention calibration failure. 
Deﬁnition 2.  According to  Tonmoy et al. (2024), hallucinations refer 
to LLMs’ behavior of making false but conﬁdent claims which are either 
impossible to verify or are contradicted by known information. They 
classify more than twenty examples of such behavior depending on tasks 
and models used. 
Deﬁnition 3.  According to  Pesaranghader and Li (2026), there are 
four categories of detections fact, faithful, logic, and time hallucination, 
and each of which needs to be tackled diﬀerently. 
Deﬁnition 4.  According to  Anh-Hoang et al. (2025), hallucinations 
can be understood as output varying systematically based on prompting 
approach but not based on the actual facts, highlighting the impact of 
how the input is formulated on the rate of hallucinations generated in 
diﬀerent types of tasks. 
Based on the above deﬁnitions, it becomes clear that hallucination 
is a multidimensional concept consisting of, at least, four kinds of er-
rors: factual hallucination, where an assertion is contradicted by world 
facts; faithful hallucination, where an assertion is inconsistent with the 
provided input; logical hallucination, where an assertion contradicts it-
self; and temporal hallucination, where date information is inaccurate.

3.2.  Diﬀerences between hallucination and related terms
The terms hallucination, misinformation, factual error, and unfaithful 
generation are often used interchangeably within the literature covered 
in this survey, but they denote related yet distinct concepts that require 
diﬀerent approaches to veriﬁcation.
Hallucination is a property of the generation process where the output 
of a generative model contains unsupported or contradictory to training 
knowledge, input, and itself information, regardless of whether it hap-
pens to be a true claim in the real world. Factual error is a property of a 
claim – a false claim about veriﬁable knowledge of the real world, unre-
lated to its source. Misinformation – as addressed in the misinformation 
detection literature surveyed in Section 4 – is a property of circulating 
content, regardless of its origin and intent, and includes disinformation, 
human factual errors, and AI hallucinations, among others. Misinforma-
tion detection is thus a superset problem that cannot be solved solely 
with the help of hallucination veriﬁcation techniques. Unfaithful gener-
ation is, in turn, a property of context-grounded generation tasks such as 
summarization or RAG: the output is considered unfaithful if it contra-
dicts the given source or evidence, even if it happens to be a factually 
correct claim about the world – i.e., a faithful summary can reproduce 
a factual error from its source without being unfaithful, while an un-
faithful summary can include a factually correct claim not present in 
the source. The primary concern of this survey, in accordance with the 
deﬁnition in Section 1, is hallucination in the former sense (a generation 
process property), while factual error and unfaithful generation veriﬁ-
cation serve as operational measures for this property, with the misin-
formation detection being a related adjacent ﬁeld with useful credibility 
assessment techniques (Section 3.4).
3.3.  Detection, veriﬁcation, mitigation, and correction: deﬁnition of 
pipeline stages
The terms detection, veriﬁcation, mitigation, and correction are used 
together in many studies about hallucination, but they correspond to 
distinct stages of the veriﬁcation process each having a unique goal, 
input, and output. This survey employs the following explicit deﬁnitions 
consistently in the remaining part of the paper.
Detection is an identiﬁcation of the generated content suspected to 
be a hallucination, but not conﬁrmed or disproven against evidence. De-
tection usually involves the analysis of internal or external generation 
process signals, such as model uncertainty, consistency of multiple out-
puts (Manakul et al., 2023), or consistency with a retrieved passage, and 
generates a ﬂag or conﬁdence score rather than a deﬁnitive judgement.
Veriﬁcation is a process of checking of a ﬂagged (or unﬂagged) 
claim against the evidence and determining if it is supported, refuted, 
or unveriﬁable. Veriﬁcation extends directly the fact-checking pipeline 
proposed by Guo et al. (2022) (claim detection, evidence retrieval, ver-
dict prediction) and performs an evidence-grounded evaluation of the 
claim, usually with the help of NLI entailment scoring (Fabbri et al., 
2022; Durmus et al., 2020; He et al., 2020) or knowledge-graph valida-
tion (Wang et al., 2020; Shami et al., 2026).
Mitigation refers to the interventions made during or before the gen-
eration process that reduces the likelihood of hallucinating in the ﬁrst 
place, rather than checking the content afterwards. RAG (Lewis et al., 
2020; Asai et al., 2023), instruction tuning with RLHF (Ouyang et al., 
2022), and DPO-based preference alignment (Rafailov et al., 2023) are 
examples of mitigation techniques – they lower the base rate of hallu-
cination but do not prevent hallucination in any particular output (Niu 
et al., 2024).
Correction is the post-generation re-writing of the output to elimi-
nate unsupported claims, once they have been ﬂagged and veriﬁed. The 
RARR framework (Gao et al., 2023a) exempliﬁes a correction technique 
that retrieves evidence for or against a generated claim and re-writes it 
accordingly.
Table 3 shows how individual techniques covered in this survey are 
mapped to the pipeline stage(s) they solve, and demonstrates that most 
of them address one or two of the four stages only, which is the reason 
for the multi-layer architectures described in Section 7.
3.4.  Fact veriﬁcation
Fact-checking, deﬁned as the task of determining whether a certain 
claim can be substantiated, rejected, or neither based on evidence, has 
been investigated intensively within the NLP literature before the advent 
of LLMs. Guo et al. (2022) set up the gold-standard three-step pipeline 
for automatic fact-checking: detecting claims, retrieving evidence, and 
making judgment predictions. This pipeline has motivated a number of 
hallucination veriﬁcation architectures (Schlichtkrull et al., 2023; Putta 
et al., 2025; Gong et al., 2026).
Some of the research on extending the fundamental pipeline is as fol-
lows. Schlichtkrull et al. (2023) created AVerITeC, which is a dataset for 
real-world claim veriﬁcation through multi-hop web evidence retrieval 
and sets the benchmark for testing the end-to-end performance of ver-
iﬁcation systems. Akhtar et al. (2024) propose Ev2r metric for system-
atically analyzing the quality of evidence used to make verdicts during 
automated fact-checking. Gong et al. (2026) extend the paradigm by 
adding multiple sources and agents for evidence retrieval, showing that 
spreading the retrieval task among agents increases diversity and lowers 
biases of retrieval processes. Eldifrawi et al. (2024) address the problem 
of automated justiﬁcation for claims by arguing that fact-checking with-
out a veriﬁable justiﬁcation has limited practical value.
The issue of evidential veriﬁcation has also received attention in re-
lation to source credibility. According to Chrysidis et al. (2024), reliable

et al., 2026; Akhtar et al., 2024), treats veriﬁcation as a pipeline prob-

dicted, with recent work reﬁning the quality of each stage (multi-hop 
retrieval (Schlichtkrull et al., 2023), evidence-quality scoring (Akhtar 
et al., 2024), multi-agent retrieval (Gong et al., 2026)) or adding a justi-

by Chrysidis et al. (2024) and Srba et al. (2026), treats veriﬁcation 
as a source-credibility problem: the reliability of a verdict depends on 
the trustworthiness of the evidence source, independent of how that 
evidence was retrieved. These are not mutually exclusive framings; a 
source-credibility signal can be incorporated as an additional weighting 
factor within the verdict-prediction stage of the pipeline view. This sur-
vey adopts the pipeline view of Guo et al. (2022) as the structural back-
bone for the veriﬁcation stage deﬁned in Section 3.3, augmented with 
source-credibility weighting (Chrysidis et al., 2024; Srba et al., 2026) 
and cross-lingual claim detection (Panchendrarajan and Zubiaga, 2026) 
as reﬁnements within that pipeline, rather than as a separate competing 
framework. This adopted framing is used consistently in the compara-
tive analysis of Section 7.
3.5.  Trustworthy AI
The concept of trustworthy AI encompasses reliability, interpretabil-
ity, fairness, safety, and alignment with human values as fundamental 
design requirements for AI systems (Amodei et al., 2016; Bommasani 
et al., 2021; Weidinger et al., 2021). For generative AI speciﬁcally, veri-
ﬁable hallucination detection is a necessary condition for achieving such 
trustworthy systems.
The paper by Bommasani et al. (2021) discusses how foundation 
models create social-scale propagation of training errors, and therefore 
calls for external veriﬁcation middleware as a necessary safety layer. 
Weidinger et al. (2021) consider ethical and social implications of LLMs 
on several levels of potential harms, including generation of mislead-
ing content, thus showing the ethical necessity of hallucination miti-
gation. Thorne Thorne (2024) establishes that interpretability is a key 
prerequisite for correct user trust estimation, arguing that a single ag-
gregate score is insuﬃcient  for users to make reasonable decisions on 
AI-generated content. Jeong et al. (2025) propose a framework for ana-
lyzing ethics and trustworthiness of generative AI systems based on sev-
eral critical aspects including factual accuracy, transparency, fairness, 
and safety.
Principles on which Amodei et al. (2016) base their work on the 
topic of AI safety can be applied to the research on hallucination veri-
ﬁcation since they discuss issues connected to side eﬀects of AI, in case 
if the system is working under misaligned conﬁdence. Hendrycks et al. 
(2020) explore more general topic, concerning issues connected to the 
alignment of AI with common human values, thus, hallucination being 
one of the problems connected with such an alignment.
The above perspectives of trustworthy AI can be categorized into 
three diﬀerent but intersecting framings rather than a single deﬁnition. 
The societal-risk framing of Bommasani et al. (2021) and Weidinger et al. 
(2021) sees trustworthiness as an attribute of deployment at scale, focus-
ing on the risks a foundation model can cause through the propagation 
of the errors it makes. The interpretability framing of Thorne (2024) and 
Jeong et al. (2025) regards trustworthiness as an attribute of the ver-
iﬁcation output, focusing on the question of whether a user is able to 
comprehend and calibrate their trust in the veriﬁcation result appro-
priately. The alignment framing of Amodei et al. (2016) and  Hendrycks 
et al. (2020) sees trustworthiness as an attribute of the training objec-
tive of the model, asking whether the behavior of the system reﬂects 
the human values intended. In this survey, none of the above framings 
are chosen exclusively; on the contrary, the approach is to view hal-
lucination veriﬁcation as an essential but alone insuﬃcient  element of 
trustworthy AI, while choosing the interpretability framing of Thorne 
(2024) speciﬁcally for veriﬁcation output (Section 8) and the societal-
risk framing of Bommasani et al. (2021) for the argument made in this 
survey about model-agnostic veriﬁcation middleware (Section 1).
3.6.  Reliability assessment
Reliability assessment in the context of LLM outputs refers to the 
systematic quantiﬁcation of the degree to which generated content can 
be trusted to be factually accurate and contextually appropriate. Several 
approaches have been proposed in the literature.
As shown by Ramprasad and Wallace (2024), automatic factual-
ity scoring tends to overestimate the accuracy of language models in 
domain-speciﬁc scenarios, which underscores the limitations of reliabil-
ity estimation using only one type of metrics in contrast to human assess-
ments of actual accuracy. Zhang et al. (2024a) also ﬁnd discrepancies 
between automated measures of faithfulness and human assessments of 
citation accuracy, pointing to the need for composite multi-signal relia-
bility scoring. Finally, FACTS Grounding Leaderboard created by Jacovi 
et al. (2025) provides a comparative evaluation framework that tests the 
ability of LLMs to ground their responses to long-form inputs. In their 
thorough review of faithfulness measures for assessing hallucinations in 
LLMs, published in IEEE Journal of Selected Topics in Signal Processing, 
Malin et al. (2025) conclude that there is no single metric capable of cap-
turing all aspects of factual accuracy, advocating for multi-faithfulness 
evaluation methods.

4.  State-of-the-art literature review
This section provides a comprehensive review of the principal re-
search directions in hallucination detection, evaluation, and mitigation. 
This analysis is conducted across eight dimensions, which correspond to 
the methodological lines within this ﬁeld. They include: hallucination 
taxonomy and evaluation, retrieval-augmented generation, automatic 
fact-checking, foundations of NLP, truth scoring and metrics, misinfor-
mation detection, cross-model and knowledge graph veriﬁcation, and 
self-veriﬁcation.
While Section 3 provided the conceptual framework of terms and 
taxonomies utilized in this survey (the nature of hallucination, the 
meaning of veriﬁcation and trustworthy AI, and the pipeline phase that 
the method targets), this section provides the synthesis of the relevant 
methodology literature in each of the eight dimensions: the particular 
methodologies, benchmarks, and systems that embody the previously 
deﬁned concepts, in chronological order within each dimension to illus-
trate the development of methodology. If the subsections below need to 
revisit a term introduced in Section 3 (such as hallucination taxonomy 
and the deﬁnition of trustworthy AI), then the discussion is restricted to 
the methodology-related content not provided in the latter section.
4.1.  Hallucination taxonomy and evaluation
The taxonomic categories underlying this subsection – factual, faith-
ful, logical, and temporal hallucination – were deﬁned and compared in 
Section 3.1; this subsection does not restate those deﬁnitions but instead 
traces the chronological development of the benchmarks used to opera-
tionalize them. Benchmark development became an important step to-
wards better understanding and assessing hallucinations. Speciﬁcally, 
TruthfulQA (Lin et al., 2022) created the ﬁrst benchmark by identifying 
factual failures in high-performing LLMs, while FActScore (Min et al., 
2023b) developed the method of atomic claim decomposition for pre-
cise factual evaluation. QAFactEval (Fabbri et al., 2022) and FEQA (Dur-
mus et al., 2020) were used to assess the consistency of LLM-generated 
summaries in QA paradigm. However, an important follow-up study by 
Ramprasad and Wallace (2024), Zhang et al. (2024a) showed that auto-
matic metrics overestimated performance by multiple metrics compared 
to human evaluation. This issue was addressed by several composite 
multi-metric evaluation approaches suggested by Malin et al. (2025), 
Lamsiyah et al. (2025), and the FACTS Grounding Leaderboard (Jacovi 
et al., 2025).
The approximate sizes reported above reﬂect the authors’ famil-
iarity with these widely cited benchmarks as documented in their 
original publications. Table 4 makes clear that no single benchmark 
jointly covers multiple languages, multiple domains, and end-to-end 
(detection-through-correction) evaluation: English-only, single-domain 
benchmarks such as FActScore, QAFactEval, and FEQA dominate the 
landscape, multilingual coverage is conﬁned to claim-clustering re-
sources such as MultiClaimNet rather than full veriﬁcation bench-
marks, and evaluation strategies range from human span annotation 
(RAGTruth) to LLM-judge scoring (FACTS Grounding), which are not 
directly comparable to one another. This fragmentation reinforces the 
case, developed further in Section 8, for benchmark suites that jointly 
vary language, domain, and pipeline stage rather than treating each as 
an independent axis.
4.2.  Retrieval-augmented generation
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) became one of the most ac-
tive areas in developing approaches for mitigating hallucinations. The 
basic design of the RAG architecture has been proposed by Lewis et al. 
(2020) in NeurIPS 2020. They discovered that conditioning genera-
tion on external retrieval passages greatly reduced hallucinations in 
knowledge-intensive tasks, a result corroborated by Shuster et al. (2021) 
for conversation scenarios. Further developments in RAG models in-
cluded Self-RAG (Asai et al., 2023), which allows adaptive retrieval 
through self-critiquing using reﬂection tokens, and Search-in-the-Chain 
(Xu et al., 2024), which reﬁnes answers through iterative retrieval. A 
novel framework, RAGAS (Es et al., 2024), allows automatic evaluation 
of RAG pipelines on diﬀerent dimensions including faithfulness, answer 
relevance, and retrieval recall. Moreover, recent studies have provided 
an extensive overview of RAG architectures; e.g., those presented by 
Gao et al. (2023b) and (Gupta et al., 2024). Despite these improve-
ments, however, RAG systems are not yet fully reliable. Niu et al. (2024) 
proves empirically that even well-structured RAG pipelines may hallu-
cinate if the retrieved context is inadequate or ambiguous. Wang et al. 
(2023) prove that self-knowledge estimation helps reduce unnecessary 
retrieval costs, whereas (Manakul et al., 2023) proposes a zero-resource 
detection of hallucinations using stochastic self-sampling. Moreover, the 
RARR framework (Gao et al., 2023a) aims at correcting hallucinated 
claims after generation through context retrieval. Mallen et al. (2023) 
have demonstrated that parametric and non-parametric models are vul-
nerable to long-tail factual queries, and Shi et al. (2023) have proven 
that even if factual information is present, LLMs are easily distracted by 
irrelevant context.
4.2.1.  Traditional RAG versus agentic RAG
The RAG variations mentioned above – standard RAG (Lewis et al., 
2020), Self-RAG (Asai et al., 2023), RAGAS (Es et al., 2024), and 
RAGTruth (Niu et al., 2024) – are all largely examples of what tradi-
tional retrieval entails: a static evidence set conditioned via a single pass 
of retrieval is the basis for generation with no room for re-planning of 
retrieval operations in light of intermediate reasoning failures. The dif-
ference between the traditional and agentic RAG is clearly articulated 
by Neha and Bhati Neha and Bhati (2025), where agentic RAG means 
autonomous agent(s) planning and conducting retrieval operations, in-
voking tools, reiterating retrieval in light of intermediate outputs, and 
reﬂecting on partial results before giving the ﬁnal output. When it comes 
to veriﬁcation of hallucinations, agentic RAG becomes especially rel-
evant due to the fact that, unlike in the traditional setting, evidence 
gathering here is a process that involves multiple retrieval stages and 
is not reduced to a single lookup operation: agents may ask for more 
evidence when needed, divide the claim into smaller parts and verify 
those separately, invoke specialized tools like calculators, knowledge 
graph searches, or citation checkers during reﬂection before giving the 
ﬁnal verdict (Neha and Bhati, 2025). The Search-In-The-Chain frame-
work (Xu et al., 2024) and the multi-agent evidence-retrieval system by 
Gong et al. (2026) may both be considered an early example of agentic 
RAG in the context of hallucination veriﬁcation research, despite the 
fact that agentic RAG was not explicitly stated. Agentic RAG should be 
preferred over traditional one when complex multi-hop reasoning is de-
sired while the latter works better in terms of latency and simplicity; 
however, agentic RAG requires more inference calls (Neha and Bhati, 
2025), which directly aﬀects our scalability discussions in Section 8.
4.3.  Automated fact-checking

based methodology for hallucination veriﬁcation. The foundational

the ﬁeld with web-sourced and multi-agent heterogeneous source meth-
ods for evidence retrieval respectively. The Akhtar et al. (2024) iden-
tiﬁed a critical gap by evaluating the evidence retrieval phase in the 
fact-checking pipeline. Recent research has enhanced and broadened the

4.4.  Natural language processing foundations
The NLP principles underlying the technology to detect hallucina-
tions rely on the transformer model designed by Vaswani et al. (2017), 
with its multi-headed self-attention feature which makes the model ca-
pable of capturing long-term dependencies necessary to decompose the 
claim and assess the implications. Bidirectional pre-training was shown 
to be important for ﬁne-tuning BERT (Devlin et al., 2019) for NLI and 
NER; Liu et al. (2019) enhanced pre-training procedures while He et al. 
(2020), with its disentangled attention mechanism, is currently the best-
performing model for detecting entailments. Larger generative models 
such as Brown et al. (2020), Achiam et al. (2023), Touvron et al. (2023) 
play a dual role as sources of hallucinations in need of veriﬁcation and 
references in cross-model consensus approaches (Adlakha et al., 2024). 
Finally, certain training practices were reported to impact hallucina-
tion rates within large language models. For instance, instruction tun-
ing with RLHF (Ouyang et al., 2022) as well as direct optimization of 
preferences (Rafailov et al., 2023) lead to lower hallucination frequen-
cies via preference alignment. Chain-of-thought prompting (Wei et al., 
2022b) contributes to better structured multi-step reasoning and thus to 
more reliable fact-checking of complicated statements. The HELM eval-
uation system (Liang et al., 2022) oﬀers a holistic view of the evaluation 
process by integrating factuality among other dimensions. (Min et al., 
2023a; Zhao et al., 2023) survey pre-training progress relevant for ver-
iﬁcation applications.
Moreover, the exact architectural features and alignments that have 
been designed into each LLM family also aﬀect how likely the model 
will be to hallucinate and how suitable it is for hallucination veriﬁ-
cation purposes. Bhati et al. (2026) conduct a comparative survey of 
the major architectures of decoder-only models such as GPT, LLaMA 2, 
Gemini, Claude, DeepSeek, Falcon, and Qwen, systematically analyzing

4.5.  Misinformation detection
The literature on misinformation detection and veriﬁcation includes 
numerous methodological resources and conceptual frames that can be 
applied directly to the problem of hallucination detection. Srba et al. 
(2026) present an extensive overview of automatic credibility assess-
ment based on textual data which provides solid theoretical ground for 
the development of multi-layer credibility veriﬁcation systems. Nguea-
jio et al. (2025) experimentally show that transformer models always 
outperform traditional machine learning algorithms in the problem of 
detecting fakes. In turn, Raza et al. (2025), Qazi et al. (2025) general-
ize this result to various languages and corpora, proving that evidence-
based retrieval works better than generation in cases of large-scale fake 
news detection. Beer and Matthee (2020), Thibault et al. (2025) of-
fer overviews of detection methodology for the evaluation of credibil-
ity. Sociological and psychological aspects of misinformation are also 
relevant for developing the veriﬁcation system. Automation bias docu-
mented by Beauvais (2022), Adams et al. (2023) causes users, especially 
non-experts within a particular domain, to blindly trust algorithmic out-
puts, making the interpretability feature crucial for credibility veriﬁca-
tion. Broda and Strömbäck (2024) provide interdisciplinarity-supported 
evidence that AI-based misinformation systematically decreases trust 
within institutions. Domain-related and regional examples of AI-based 
fake content spread are provided by Tomassi et al. (2025), Kishwar and 
Zafar (2023), respectively. Multimodal applications of AI-based fakes in 
a cloud environment are studied by Wang et al. (2024), Abduljaleel and 
Ali (2024), Cavus et al. (2024).
Misinformation-detection models presented in the preceding para-
graphs either separate the two tasks into retrieval and fake-news classi-
ﬁcation or consider them separately for RAG and classiﬁcation. Systems 
that integrate the fact-checking process based on retrieval-augmented 
generation architecture provide a closer integration of these two tasks. 
For instance, TrumorGPT (Hang et al., 2025) proposes to combine 
a language model with graph-based retrieval-augmented generation 
(GraphRAG) on constantly refreshed semantic health knowledge graphs 
for fact-checking of health claims, tackling the problem of hallucinations 
at the fact-checking stage rather than only considering article classiﬁ-
cation as fake or real. Similarly, Bai and Fu (2024) present FCRV (Full-
Context Retrieval and Veriﬁcation), in which a fact-checking task is inte-
grated through LLM-based extraction of claims and evidence retrieval by 
RAG to build “full-context” representation of the news article prior to its 
veriﬁcation. These models demonstrate that for the hallucination fact-
checking problem in particular, a coupled pipeline approach proposed 
in Section 4.2.1 is more appropriate than approaches from the mostly 
classiﬁcation-oriented misinformation detection literature above.
4.6.  Cross-model veriﬁcation and knowledge graph approaches
The technique of cross-model veriﬁcation relies on the idea that 
independent models cannot hallucinate in the same way; thus, their 
inter-model agreement is an eﬃcient  veriﬁcation cue. Adlakha et al. 
(2024) show empirically that the scores in cross-model evaluation align 
highly with human assessments of hallucination presence. Meanwhile, 
Manakul et al. (2023) use stochastic self-sampling during generation to 
detect hallucinations at zero-resource conditions, called SelfCheckGPT. 
Shami et al. (2026) prove the eﬃcacy  of using SPARQL-NLI methods in 
verifying knowledge graphs’ factuality, speciﬁcally in entity-attribute 
consistency tasks. Knowledge graph-based techniques are beneﬁcial ad-
ditions to retrieval-based approaches as they oﬀer symbolic and explain-
able paths to veriﬁcation. According to Wang et al. (2020), cross-graph 
representation learning provides the theoretical framework behind the 
approach to entity-attribute consistency veriﬁcation in pipelines. Huang 
et al. (2025b) apply a similar principle to verify factuality of a statement 
using graph-based fact checking and propagation of evidence along 
multi-hop relational chains. Song et al. (2025) achieve promising re-
sults with energy networks that perform set-consistency veriﬁcation in 
a structured manner.
4.7.  Trustworthy AI and ethical frameworks
The normative foundations of trustworthy AI relevant to hallucina-
tion veriﬁcation – the societal-risk, interpretability, and alignment fram-
ings, and the framework this survey adopts from them – are established 
in Section 3.5 and are not repeated here. The literature-speciﬁc addi-
tion this subsection contributes is empirical rather than deﬁnitional: 
Perez et al. (2022) show by adversarial red-teaming experiments that 
even properly aligned models can be made to generate hallucinations if 
prompted correctly, demonstrating that the alignment framing of Sec-
tion 3.5 (Amodei et al., 2016; Hendrycks et al., 2020) is necessary but 
not suﬃcient  on its own, and that red-teaming of veriﬁcation systems – 
not just of the underlying generative model – is an important comple-
mentary safeguard, a point returned to in the future-directions discus-
sion of Section 9.2.
5.  Applications of hallucination detection systems
Hallucination detection and mitigation techniques are highly rele-
vant to many ﬁelds, where factual accuracy plays a crucial role. This 
section covers key application areas where such techniques have been 
investigated or applied, providing domain-speciﬁc details based on the 
existing literature. Fig. 2 depicts the key application domains for hallu-
cination detection systems.
5.1.  Healthcare and clinical decision support
Healthcare emerges as a critical domain of applications for the pur-
pose of verifying hallucinations. Using AI content in clinical decision 
support, patient interactions, and literature summarization poses seri-
ous risks, especially in case of possible hallucinations. Multiple studies 
show that guidelines or medical information generated by LLMs con-
tain proven hallucinations (Beauvais, 2022; Adams et al., 2023), posing 
a clear threat to patient health and safety.
The current body of literature states that the prevalence of halluci-
nations in medical domains signiﬁcantly exceeds general QA scenarios 
for queries regarding specialized clinical knowledge (Pesaranghader and 
Li, 2026; Kaddour et al., 2023). Such increase can be explained by the 
long tail distribution of specialized knowledge compared to general pre-
training datasets, as proven by Mallen et al. (2023). In their work, they 
showed that parametric memories cannot be considered reliable sources 
of knowledge within a domain underrepresented in training corpora. 
While RAG systems perform promisingly in specialized domains, by us-
ing the context from specialized literature (Lewis et al., 2020; Asai et al.,

5.2.  Legal and judicial applications
The ﬁeld of law also entails similarly high stakes. It has been reported 
that LLMs are capable of inventing cases, misattribution of legal prece-
dents, and creating a legally valid but factually erroneous summary of 
a statute (Kaddour et al., 2023; Adams et al., 2023). The repercussions 
associated with such a phenomenon for legal practice can be signiﬁcant, 
ranging from punishment for lawyers to miscarriages of justice during 
adversarial proceedings.
Current methods of verifying facts in a legal context have involved 
adopting the established three-step process of fact checking (Guo et al., 
2022) and using ontology or knowledge graph techniques tailored to 
legal documents (Wang et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2025b). Shami et al. 
(2026) prove that knowledge graph validation techniques are highly 
useful for entity-attribute consistency tasks in legal texts, especially be-
cause accurate attribution of a holding to a court and date is very im-
portant. Eldifrawi et al. (2024) highlight the importance of automated

In the legal context, the evidence sources that are most relevant in

case law, statute and regulations databases, and court docket records,

getting the individual involved in trouble, the literature presented in 
this survey indicates the tolerance of error in the claims about citation 
existence and holding attribution as equal to zero or close to zero.
5.3.  Scientiﬁc research and academic integrity
In scientiﬁc research, hallucination is manifested through the inclu-
sion of ﬁctitious citations, incorrect attribution of statistics, and erro-
neous representations of experimental results  (Huang et al., 2025a; Pe-
saranghader and Li, 2026). The rapid proliferation of LLM-based writing 
support tools within academia has caused a great concern regarding hal-
lucination in scientiﬁc text, prompting institutions such as journal edi-
tors and research integrity oﬃces  to develop strategies for hallucination 
detection (Amodei et al., 2016; Weidinger et al., 2021).
FActScore (Min et al., 2023b) was developed for the speciﬁc purpose 
of measuring the precision of facts in text generation and can be used di-
rectly to verify scientiﬁc text. Muhlgay et al. (2024) propose techniques 
to automatically generate benchmarks for evaluating factuality in lan-
guage models, creating scalable means for assessing LLMs for factual 
information. FACTS Grounding Leaderboard (Jacovi et al., 2025) is a 
benchmark for measuring an LLM’s capability for grounding text based 
on input documents.
For scientiﬁc writing, the main sources of evidence are peer-
reviewed publication databases and citation indices (such as the indexed 
literature on which Scopus and Web of Science rely), which allow one 
to verify the existence, authorship, and claims made in an artiﬁcial cita-
tion (Min et al., 2023b; Muhlgay et al., 2024). Since artiﬁcial citations 
and misattributions of statistics jeopardize research integrity and could 
contaminate the scientiﬁc record if not identiﬁed, the literature shows 
that there should be close to zero tolerance for such mistakes when it 
comes to citation existence and statistical attribution in the context of 
scientiﬁc writing.
5.4.  Education and information access
Application areas of LLMs within education such as AI-powered tu-
toring systems, automated question answering, and curriculum content 
generation face potential risks of hallucinations that are especially harm-
ful to users who lack suﬃcient  background to recognize the errors of the 
model (Kaddour et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2022). Hallucination poses 
an especially big problem in the ﬁeld due to the population asymme-
try where the users do not have the required competence to critically 
evaluate the information presented by LLMs, which can lead to learning 
incorrect data by heart.
Frameworks used to detect misinformation (Beer and Matthee, 2020; 
Cavus et al., 2024; Thibault et al., 2025) have oﬀered approaches that 
can be used in the context of educational moderation to detect explana-
tions with factual errors regarding science and history.  Beauvais (2022), 
Adams et al. (2023) have found that automation bias the inclination to 
take the outputs from an algorithm without questioning them is higher 
among users who have lower expertise within the speciﬁc domain.
In the context of education, the appropriate sources of evidence are 
already accepted curriculum standards, peer-reviewed textbooks, and 
reference material on the subject matter in question, as these provide 
the “ground truth” that must be reproduced by the tutoring/curriculum 
generation system (Beer and Matthee, 2020; Cavus et al., 2024). As the 
users of the tutoring system lack domain knowledge and therefore would 
be unlikely to detect errors made in the learning process, as well as the 
fact that incorrect information may become internalized by the learner, 
it is recommended to set a low level of tolerance for factual mistakes 
(dates, deﬁnitions, procedures) but higher tolerance for pedagogically 
open-ended statements.
5.5.  Public information and journalism
The deployment of LLMs in news generation, content summarization, 
and public information dissemination creates macro-level hallucination 
risks that extend to societal trust in information ecosystems.  Broda and 
Strömbäck (2024) have shown that the spread of misinformation under-
mines people’s trust in journalism and democratic institutions. Tomassi 
et al. (2025) report how AI disinformation has been propagated within 
the discussion on climate science. Cavus et al. (2024) have developed 
a cloud-based system to detect fake news in online social networks in 
real-time.
6.  Enabling technologies
Several technological enablers support the creation of hallucination 
veriﬁcation systems. Similarly to the industrial technological enablers 
required to realize advanced production concepts (Lewis et al., 2020), 
veriﬁcation technology could be considered the basic components from 
which multi-tier detection structures are created. The following is an 
overview of the key technological enablers discussed, presented in Ta-
ble 5 and Fig. 3.
6.1.  Transformer architectures and pre-trained language models
Building on the transformer architecture of Vaswani et al. (2017), 
the speciﬁc capability that transformer-based models contribute to hal-
lucination veriﬁcation is self-attention-based contextual representation 
of long-range dependencies, which is a necessary representational capa-
bility for claim decomposition, entailment analysis, and entity extrac-
tion in veriﬁcation processes; the general architecture itself is not re-
derived here, as it is well established in the NLP literature. The impor-
tance of bidirectional pre-training for NLI ﬁne-tuning was illustrated by 
BERT (Devlin et al., 2019); RoBERTa (Liu et al., 2019) improved model 
robustness; and DeBERTa (He et al., 2020) utilized disentangled atten-
tion, allowing for the best publicly available framework for entailment-
based hallucination detection.
Larger models like GPT-3 (Brown et al., 2020), GPT-4 (Achiam et al., 
2023), and LLaMA-2 (Touvron et al., 2023) play a double role in hal-
lucination validation by providing the hallucinated text that needs to 
be validated and being used as the reference model for a multi-model 
consensus-based approach (Adlakha et al., 2024). Instruction tuning 
based on RLHF (Ouyang et al., 2022) and DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) is 
an eﬀective strategy in lowering the number of hallucinations by pref-
erence alignment. A detailed cross-family comparison of these archi-
tectural and alignment choices, and their implications for hallucination 
veriﬁcation, is provided in Section 3.4 (Bhati et al., 2026).
6.2.  Dense retrieval and retrieval-augmented generation
Retrieval-based techniques for hallucination mitigation using RAG 
embed queries and documents into common vector spaces based on 
semantic similarity for retrieval. These dense retrieval systems repre-
sent the core of current RAG-based techniques for mitigating halluci-
nations (Lewis et al., 2020; Asai et al., 2023). The main breakthrough 
achieved by the RAG architecture for addressing hallucinations is the 
exchange of parametric knowledge (which is prone to hallucinations) 
stored within the weights of the NLP model with non-parametric knowl-
edge retrieved at inference time from external sources. This technique 
has been shown to eﬀectively reduce the error rates in knowledge-
intensive NLP tasks by  Lewis et al. (2020). The Self-RAG technique (Asai 
et al., 2023) further improves upon this approach by enabling models

6.3.  Knowledge graphs and structured veriﬁcation
Graphs of Knowledge – structured databases comprised of entities, 
attributes, and relations – allow the veriﬁcation of claims by LLMs using 
symbolic veriﬁcation based on grounded assertions. Wang et al. (2020) 
show how cross-graph representation learning can be used for knowl-
edge graph validation, which constitutes the theoretical underpinning 
for the use of attribute-entity consistency veriﬁcation in veriﬁcations 
pipelines. Huang et al. (2025b) propose an approach to graph-based 
veriﬁcation that is speciﬁcally tailored to fact-checking, utilizing rela-
tional structure in order to propagate evidence through multi-hop claim 
chains.  Shami et al. (2026) compare LLMs for knowledge graph veriﬁca-
tion, identifying a hybrid approach consisting of SPARQL-based query-
ing and NLI entailments as the most accurate.  Song et al. (2025) devise 
set-consistency energy networks, achieving competitive results in the 
context of structured consistency veriﬁcation for multi-hop veriﬁcation 
tasks.
The use of knowledge graphs within hallucination veriﬁcation sys-
tems brings several strengths when compared to retrieval-based tech-
niques, including computational eﬃciency  for the veriﬁcation of entity-
attribute assertions, provision of traceable evidence pathways, and im-
munity to failure due to retrieval issues based on improper query formu-
lation. Existing challenges include the insuﬃcient  coverage of speciﬁc 
domains within publicly available knowledge graphs like Wikidata and 
the cost of computational resources to maintain current entity represen-
tations within fast-changing domains.
6.4.  Natural language inference and entailment scoring
The task of natural language inference (NLI), where it is needed to 
determine if a premise contradicts, entails, or is neutral with respect to a 
hypothesis, is an integral part of hallucination detection. Within the con-
text of veriﬁcation systems, the statement to be veriﬁed is the hypothesis 
and the gathered evidence is the premise. The entailment probability is 
an appropriate method of evaluating the consistency between the two 
and the degree of the factual relationship. DeBERTa (He et al., 2020), 
which was ﬁne-tuned on MultiNLI dataset, provides cutting-edge per-
formance when used for entailment scoring within the context of ver-
iﬁcation, reaching high accuracy when processing scientiﬁc and news 
domain texts.  Zaman and Srivastava (2025) perform causal analysis 
of faithfulness metrics, ﬁnding factors which negatively inﬂuence the 
evaluation when only using NLI scores.
However, several papers (Pesaranghader and Li, 2026; Malin et al., 
2025) ﬁnd out that although the NLI-based entailment scoring is eﬃ-
cient in identifying factual hallucinations when there is unambiguous 
counter-evidence provided, it is rather unreliable when dealing with 
faithful and logical hallucinations with implicit contradiction or multi-
hop reasoning.
6.5.  Credibility scoring and source authority assessment
Evidence found in support of the claim and used for claim veriﬁca-
tion becomes reliable or unreliable based on the reliability of the ev-
idence source. Srba et al. (2026) present an extensive review of auto-
matic credibility assessment based on textual credibility cues, outlin-
ing the taxonomy of authority markers relevant to hallucination veri-
ﬁcation scenarios. Chrysidis et al. (2024) show that the ability to dif-
ferentiate between credible and noncredible, or even leaked, pieces 
of evidence greatly boosts the accuracy of automated verdicts. Akhtar 
et al. (2024) propose the Ev2r measure as an evaluation measure 
for evidence retrieval-based fact-checking pipelines. Thus, the above-
mentioned works prove that the authority of sources can be considered 
a valuable claim veriﬁcation signal.

suggests a certain claim is true while an NLI model ﬁnds a contradic-

a knowledge-graph evaluation. Three conﬂict resolution strategies can 
be inferred from the layer-speciﬁc approaches discussed elsewhere in 
this survey despite the fact that none of the surveyed systems employ 
all three: (i) precedence rules - a form of hard-wired policy that makes 
a certain type of signal more authoritative over other types when re-

knowledge-graph validation for entity-attribute claims (Section 6.3) due 
to its higher rating for the type of hallucination (Table 5), and prece-

rather than a binary classiﬁcation, and combining these uncertainties 
using either a weighted aggregation (which could be optimized in a 
data-driven fashion as suggested in Section 9.2), or a Bayesian one; and 
(iii) human review, i.e., escalation of conﬂict resolution to a human ex-
pert in case the degree of disagreement between the signals passes a 
certain threshold. In the absence of a validated conﬂict resolution algo-
rithm in the current literature, the latter is the most conservative and 
currently best-supported default approach in high-stakes settings (Sec-
tion 5), while the two former are more applicable in low-stakes, high-
throughput situations.
7.2.  Retrieval-grounded versus model-internal approaches
Another important dichotomy is between retrieval-grounded veriﬁ-
cation, which grounds its claims on externally retrieved information, 
and veriﬁcation methods that utilize consistency signals generated by 
the model itself. RAG models (Lewis et al., 2020; Asai et al., 2023; Gao 
et al., 2023b) and fact-checking pipelines (Guo et al., 2022; Schlichtkrull 
et al., 2023; Putta et al., 2025) fall under the former category while 
self-consistency-based methods (Manakul et al., 2023) and model self-
evaluation (Pesaranghader and Li, 2026) belong to the latter one.
While veriﬁcation methods based on externally retrieved informa-
tion beneﬁt from independence from biased knowledge representation 
of the generating model, their performance is limited by retrieval qual-
ity and coverage (Mallen et al., 2023; Niu et al., 2024). Model internal 
veriﬁcation approaches, on the other hand, can identify hallucinations 
independently of external APIs but suﬀer from correlated errors due 
to consistent model misrepresentations in certain domains (Bommasani 
et al., 2021; Shami et al., 2026). Current literature points to the beneﬁts 
of hybrid approaches that prefer retrieval-based signals to cross-model 
consensus in detecting diverse hallucinations.
The model-agnostic framing adopted throughout this survey (Sec-
tion 1) refers speciﬁcally to parameter access: post-generation middle-
ware of this kind does not require access to the generating model’s 
weights, training data, or internal activations. This should not be read 
as implying that such middleware is agnostic to the generating model 
in every respect. In practice, the eﬀectiveness of externally applied ver-
iﬁcation still depends on properties of the generating model’s output: its 
verbosity and formatting conventions aﬀect how cleanly a response can 
be decomposed into independently veriﬁable claims (Min et al., 2023b); 
its citation behavior (whether and how it attributes claims to sources) 
determines how much veriﬁcation work the middleware must perform 
versus can oﬄoad  to the model itself; and its output language deter-
mines which retrieval, NLI, and knowledge-graph resources are avail-
able downstream, a constraint discussed further in Section 8 on mul-
tilingual coverage. Consequently, “model-agnostic” is more accurately 
understood as parameter-agnostic but not output-agnostic: the middleware 
can be applied to any generating model without modiﬁcation to that 
model, but its practical performance is still shaped by that model’s out-
put characteristics, and this distinction should be kept in mind when 
comparing veriﬁcation systems across diﬀerent generating models.
7.3.  Factuality metrics: automated versus human evaluation
The diﬀerence between automated metrics of factuality and human 
judgment is an enduring problem in the literature. As  Ramprasad and 
Wallace (2024) show, automated metrics tend to overestimate the ac-
curacy of large language models, while  Zhang et al. (2024a) report 
contradictions between automated measures of faithfulness and human 
citation judgment. According to the literature reviews by  Lamsiyah et al. 
(2025) and Malin et al. (2025), there is no automated metric that covers 
all aspects of factual accuracy in diﬀerent application areas and tasks.
Such evidence inspires the idea of combining multiple automated 
metrics with human assessment on sample datasets (Jacovi et al., 2025). 
The results of causality analysis conducted by  Zaman and Srivastava 
(2025) indicate that some factors aﬀecting the evaluation of a sin-
gle metric may lead to the wrong estimate of the model reliability. 
HELM (Liang et al., 2022) is currently the most ambitious framework 
for evaluating diﬀerent metrics such as factuality, toxicity, fairness, and 
eﬃciency  at once.
7.4.  Domain speciﬁcity and generalization
Diﬀerent solutions exhibit wide variations in terms of domain. 
General-purpose veriﬁcation solutions like TruthfulQA (Lin et al., 2022), 
AVerITeC (Schlichtkrull et al., 2023), and RAGAS (Es et al., 2024), 
for example, oﬀer extensive benchmarking capabilities but might fail 
to accommodate the specialized nature of knowledge required by ver-
iﬁcation in medical, legal or scientiﬁc settings. On the other hand, 
domain-speciﬁc approaches such as solutions designed to detect fake 
news within particular regions (Kishwar and Zafar, 2023) and to ver-
ify multimodal content in e-commerce (Liu et al., 2024) exhibit greater 
precision but lack generalizability.
The recent body of work shows that the choice of the retrieval 
sources has a strong impact on the ability of a RAG solution to handle 
various domains: systems with access to specialized academic resources 
and institution-speciﬁc repositories outperform other systems at detect-
ing domain-speciﬁc hallucinations signiﬁcantly (Chrysidis et al., 2024; 
Srba et al., 2026). These ﬁndings suggest that scalable domain-speciﬁc 
hallucination detection requires customized retrieval sources tailored to 
specialized knowledge repositories, rather than improvements to base 
model architectures alone.
8.  Open challenges and research gaps
Despite advancements in detecting and mitigating hallucinations, 
there are certain challenges within the research literature that consti-
tute the current frontier of research within the ﬁeld. The following is a 
synthesis of these key open challenges and research gaps.
8.1.  Scalability and computational eﬃciency
The multi-layer veriﬁcation architectures that achieve the best de-
tection performance are computationally expensive, typically requiring 
multiple API calls to large generative models, dense vector retrieval 
across large corpora, and knowledge graph lookups (Gao et al., 2023b; 
Shami et al., 2026). The end-to-end delay latency of existing frame-
works often surpasses ﬁve seconds, making it impractical for real-time 
generation processes as well as browser-based applications (Asai et al., 
2023; Manakul et al., 2023). According to the current literature, evi-
dence caching with time-to-live (TTL) expiration policies for commonly 
veriﬁed themes, asynchronous processing of veriﬁcation stages, and fast 
mode for certain claims can be considered promising techniques for low-
ering latency costs (Es et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024).

8.2.  Temporal inconsistency and knowledge staleness
Temporal hallucinations, which involve LLMs stating facts that are 
valid at training time but subsequently become obsolete, constitute an 
especially tricky type of hallucination that is poorly handled by most 
current solutions (Pesaranghader and Li, 2026; Drchal et al., 2024). 
Temporal staleness can be countered by RAGs via retrieval of current ev-
idence; however, the success of such an approach hinges on the freshness 
and scope of the retrieval corpus (Gupta et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2023b). 
Knowledge graphs are also subject to temporal staleness when attributes 
of entities are updated faster than knowledge graph updates (Wang 
et al., 2020; Shami et al., 2026). The current state-of-the-art lacks a 
robust method of detecting temporal hallucinations applicable to fast-
evolving ﬁelds like politics, ﬁnance, and medicine.
There are two failure modes that both take the form of an LLM as-
serting a now out-of-date fact, which require diﬀerent types of mitiga-
tion strategies. Proper temporal hallucination, as outlined in Table 2, 
is where an LLM asserts an incorrect date, duration, or chronological 
order, often arising from a training-time snapshot that is not kept up-
to-date. A second, distinct type of failure is the retrieval of outdated but 
formerly correct evidence, where a RAG or veriﬁcation process ﬁnds a 
passage that was accurate at its time of publication, but which has since 
been rendered obsolete by later events (for example, an outdated clini-
cal guideline, a repealed statute, or a superseded oﬃce-holder  record), 
and the veriﬁcation system mistakenly marks the information as still be-
ing current. The ﬁrst is a property of the generating LLM’s knowledge 
base; the second is a property of the retrieved evidence itself and doesn’t 
require any LLM hallucinations at all. Since the second failure mode is 
not addressed by conditioning the generation process on the retrieved 
facts alone, there are three speciﬁc types of mitigations found in the 
wider literature on RAG and knowledge graphs that are directly appli-
cable: timestamped retrieval, where evidence is indexed and ranked by 
recency and where older-than-threshold passages are ignored or down-
weighed; temporal knowledge graphs, where entity attributes are stored 
with versioning information, allowing a veriﬁcation system to distin-
guish between facts that were true as of some past 𝑡, and those that are 
true now (Wang et al., 2020; Shami et al., 2026); and evidence expiry 
and re-veriﬁcation policies, where claims are assigned a validity period 
after which they are expired and must be re-veriﬁed rather than being 
treated as permanently veriﬁed. No systems found in Table 6 use all 
three forms of mitigation together, which is highlighted by this survey 
as an important area for future research and benchmarking.
8.3.  Interpretability and explainability
Present systems for detecting hallucinations generate verdicts about 
their validity that remain inaccessible to the lay user; they provide an ag-
gregated measure of reliability without specifying the particular claims 
that were ﬂagged and the reason for ﬂagging (Jeong et al., 2025; Thorne, 
2024). According to Thorne, interpretability is a pre-requisite for cor-
rect calibration of user trust regarding veriﬁcation output as it makes 
no sense to act responsibly when dealing with results whose meaning 
cannot be understood (Thorne, 2024). Jeong et al. show that trans-
parency and interpretability constitute basic conditions for a reliable AI 
system (Jeong et al., 2025). However, the majority of systems designed 
for hallucination detection still provide only aggregated measurements.
8.4.  Domain dependency and specialization
The performance of both retrieval-grounded and model-based veriﬁ-
cation approaches degrades substantially in specialized domains where 
training data coverage is limited and specialized knowledge is required 
for accurate veriﬁcation. Similarly,  Niu et al. (2024) argue that RAG 
models are likely to hallucinate when they rely on insuﬃciently  special-
ized information. Medical, legal, and ﬁnancial ﬁelds have their speciﬁc 
terminology, referencing style, and standard requirements for evidence, 
which are not taken into account by generalized veriﬁcation tools (Kad-
dour et al., 2023; Bommasani et al., 2021). Comprehensive domain-
adaptive veriﬁcation frameworks that systematically address these spe-
cialized requirements remain an understudied area, representing a crit-
ical gap for future research.
8.5.  Multilingual coverage
The vast amount of research regarding hallucination detection has 
been carried out in English with multilingual approaches receiving much 
less focus (Panchendrarajan and Zubiaga, 2026; Panchendrarajan et al., 
2025; Drchal et al., 2024). An example of work that stands out in this re-
gard is the work by  Panchendrarajan and Zubiaga (2026), who present 
cross linguistic claim detection that is aware of entities at the EACL 
2026 conference. The authors  Panchendrarajan et al. (2025) present 
MultiClaimNet as a multilingual tool to evaluate clusters of fact checked 
claims. The authors  (Drchal et al., 2024) propose an approach to auto-
mated fact checking in multiple languages.
8.6.  Multimodal hallucination
As LLMs increasingly incorporate visual, auditory, and structured 
data inputs, hallucination extends beyond text to encompass multi-
modal content. Jiang et al. (2024) propose a hallucination enhanced 
contrastive learning technique for multimodal LLMs in CVPR 2024, il-
lustrating that multimodal hallucinations happen in diﬀerent ways com-
pared to hallucinations in text-based LLMs. Zhang et al. (2024b) eval-
uate factuality on multimodal summarization, stressing the problems 
encountered in verifying multimodal hallucinations outside text.  Wang 
et al. (2024) introduce multimodal fake news detection through LLMs. 
Nevertheless, multimodal benchmark datasets that tackle all three types 
of problems together require further development.
8.7.  Privacy-preserving veriﬁcation
LLM hallucination veriﬁcation systems typically require transmitting 
user queries and AI-generated responses to external modules, including 
retrieval APIs, cross-model evaluation APIs, and knowledge graph ser-
vices (Lewis et al., 2020; Gao et al., 2023b; Shami et al., 2026). This 
process raises signiﬁcant privacy concerns in critical domains such as 
healthcare and legal practice, where user inputs may contain personally 
identiﬁable information. According to  Weidinger et al. (2021), privacy 
becomes an ethical issue with respect to LLMs, while  Bommasani et al. 
(2021) observe that while veriﬁcation modules are necessary for the 
proper functioning of LLMs, they complicate data governance consider-
ably. In the current literature, there is no architectural design for veri-
fying LLM outputs without transmitting sensitive data externally (refer 
to Table 7).
8.8.  Human-in-the-loop veriﬁcation
Most of the veriﬁcation architectures discussed in Section 7 are fully 
automated end-to-end, despite the domain-speciﬁc error-threshold anal-
ysis presented in Section 5 implying that fully automated decisions 
might be unsuitable for high-risk and truly ambiguous claims (for exam-
ple, disputed legal precedent, emerging clinical recommendation, or a 
claim contradicting evidence presented in Section 7). However, the sur-
veyed literature gives little guidance on human review integration into 
the veriﬁcation cycle. Automation bias – the phenomenon of the user, es-
pecially the non-expert user, trusting algorithmic output blindly (Beau-
vais, 2022; Adams et al., 2023) – is the key challenge here: adding a 
human reviewer to the process without showing him or her the evi-
dence underlying the verdict does not mitigate the bias, as the liter-
ature on claim-level evidence presentation and interpretability (Jeong 
et al., 2025; Thorne, 2024) emphasizes. As we adopt the concept of in-
terpretability from Thorne (Thorne, 2024) in Section 3.5, the human re-
view needs to have access not only to the aggregated conﬁdence score

8.9.  Coverage-usability trade-oﬀs and risk-adaptive veriﬁcation
An open challenge related to, but diﬀerent from the latency issue 
discussed in Section 8.1 is the trade-oﬀ between the veriﬁcation cov-
erage and usability. Independent veriﬁcation of all the atomic claims 
composing the response, as in the case of the FActScore-style atomic 
decomposition (Min et al., 2023b), increases the factual coverage of the 
response, but implementing such approach to all the claims in all the re-
sponses can fragment the response, generate excessive warnings to the 
user, degrade user experience, and increase latency and computational 
costs, as described in Section 8.1. None of the compared systems listed in 
Table 6 explicitly describe any policy of veriﬁcation depth adaptation. 
Given the domain-speciﬁc error thresholds established in Section 5, the 
solution appears obvious: risk-adaptive veriﬁcation, in which the depth 
of the claim veriﬁcation (e.g., full atomic decomposition or single holis-
tic veriﬁcation) is determined based on the domain (medical and legal 
claims deserve deeper veriﬁcation than casual information search), im-
portance of the claim to the response (the more important the claim is, 
the deeper the veriﬁcation needs to be performed), the uncertainty of the 
model (conﬁdence score), the expertise of the user (and thus the amount 
of risk that can be left on the user), and consequences of the false an-
swer being uncaught. Implementation of the risk-adaptive veriﬁcation 
in practice depends on solving the human-in-the-loop problem stated in 
Section 8.8 and conﬂicting signals handling strategy of Section 7.
9.  Lessons learned and future research directions
This section discusses the lessons learned from the current state-of-
the-art studies, and based on these lessons, synthesizes the future re-
search challenges that need to be addressed to pave the way for reli-
able, interpretable, and scalable hallucination detection and mitigation 
ecosystems in next-generation generative AI systems.
9.1.  Lessons learned
Rapid progress in generative AI has brought forward the increasing 
relevance of mechanisms aimed at hallucination detection, validation, 
and mitigation in various spheres of application such as medicine, edu-
cation, science, law, and informational services for the general public. It 
is clear from the ﬁndings of the reviewed literature that there is no uni-
versal method for detecting and validating hallucinations, and therefore 
it is crucial to develop a multi-tier system of mechanisms that include 
data retrieval, fact checking, reasoning and trustworthiness assessment 
methods. This section outlines key lessons learned from cutting-edge re-
search, technologies, and real-life implementation cases of generative 
artiﬁcial intelligence.
9.1.1.  On hallucination taxonomy
Based on the deﬁnitions and categorizations provided in the liter-
ature,it can be seen that hallucinations are multidimensional concepts 
that cannot be deﬁned by one type of error only. According to Huang 
et al. (2025a), factuality, faithfulness, and consistency are three diﬀer-
ent failures that have diﬀerent origins and requirements. In turn, Pe-
saranghader and Li (2026) have expanded the taxonomy into four types 
of errors, including factual, faithful, logical, and temporal. The common 
theme in all these studies is that the design of a detection algorithm for 
one speciﬁc error type would not work for other error types.
9.1.2.  On detection methodologies
The surveyed literature consistently shows that there exists no sin-
gle detection method that performs reliably across all types of halluci-
nation and across all application domains. RAG systems decrease but 
do not eliminate hallucinations (Niu et al., 2024); factuality metrics 
overestimate accuracy systematically (Ramprasad and Wallace, 2024); 
cross-model agreement is constrained by the extent of overlap in train-
ing data (Bommasani et al., 2021); and self-evaluating models are sub-
ject to self-referential bias (Pesaranghader and Li, 2026). One of the 
strongest lessons from the literature is that veriﬁcation systems that 
combine retrieval, cross-model agreement, and logical coherence layers 
signiﬁcantly outperform single-layer systems, a result that has been con-
ﬁrmed repeatedly in ablation studies (Manakul et al., 2023; Gao et al., 
2023a).
9.1.3.  On enabling technologies
Enabling technologies such as transformer-based NLI, dense re-
trieval, knowledge graphs, and credibility scoring each contribute dis-
tinct veriﬁcation abilities. Transformer NLI systems produce claim-level

entailment scores (Devlin et al., 2019; He et al., 2020); dense retriev-
ers ensure that veriﬁcation is grounded in current external knowl-
edge (Lewis et al., 2020; Asai et al., 2023); knowledge graphs enable 
entity-attribute consistency checks (Wang et al., 2020; Huang et al., 
2025b; Shami et al., 2026); and credibility scorers weight evidence 
based on source authority (Chrysidis et al., 2024; Srba et al., 2026). 
The use of enabling technologies in combination is what deﬁnes the 
state-of-the-art approach in hallucination veriﬁcation system design.
9.1.4.  On application domains
Analysis of veriﬁcation across various application domains suggests 
that false positive rates, hallucination types, and acceptable rates of cor-
rections diﬀer signiﬁcantly between them. In healthcare and legal do-
mains, very low false positives rates are needed to correct hallucinations, 
while information access applications can accept higher false positive 
rates in return for higher recall (Kaddour et al., 2023; Bommasani et al., 
2021; Weidinger et al., 2021). This need to account for the variation in 
performance criteria motivates the development of conﬁgurable veriﬁ-
cation systems that allow for adjusting veriﬁcation performance based 
on context.
9.2.  Future research directions
Although there has been signiﬁcant progress towards detecting and 
mitigating hallucinations in AI-generated content, there are some chal-
lenges left to be solved, such as scalability, computational eﬃciency,  
explainability, multilingual capability, privacy preservation, and mul-
timodel veriﬁcation. From reviewing the relevant studies, it is possi-
ble to conclude that existing methods have their limitations in terms 
of providing consistent, explainable, and domain-adaptive veriﬁcation 
mechanisms. Taking into account identiﬁed gaps and innovative devel-
opments, this part will address potential areas of future research for the 
development of more eﬃcient  veriﬁcation methods.
9.2.1.  Data-driven weight optimization
A limitation that is common across composite trust scoring ap-
proaches is that weight assignments are typically made based on ex-
pert judgment rather than data-driven optimization (Ramprasad and 
Wallace, 2024; Malin et al., 2025). A future research direction would 
therefore be to develop methodologies to optimize component weights 
using, for example, Bayesian regression techniques on veriﬁcation 
datasets (Muhlgay et al., 2024). Developing weight vectors for medicine, 
law, and ﬁnance would be of particular importance given the perfor-
mance degradation seen in these domains.
9.2.2.  Multilingual and cross-Lingual veriﬁcation
It is necessary to overcome the heavy English-language bias of exist-
ing research on hallucination detection through the multilingual expan-
sion of benchmarks and veriﬁcation systems. Cross-lingual entity-aware 
claim detection (Panchendrarajan and Zubiaga, 2026) and multilingual 
claim clustering (Panchendrarajan et al., 2025) lay important founda-
tions, but multilingual components for NER, NLI, and retrieval are neces-
sary for veriﬁcation systems to operate across multiple languages. Mul-
tilingual fact checking infrastructure such as MultiClaimNet (Panchen-
drarajan et al., 2025) and multilingual fact checking pipelines (Drchal 
et al., 2024) should be extended to include lower-resource languages 
absent from the literature.
9.2.3.  Real-time veriﬁcation and latency reduction
Veriﬁcation systems will have to reduce end-to-end veriﬁcation la-
tency considerably before practical deployment is possible. A promising 
avenue of future research would be the use of pre-computed embed-
dings for topics that are frequently queried, progressive user interfaces 
that provide results before full veriﬁcation has taken place, local device-
based NLI systems for privacy-sensitive use cases, and adaptive veriﬁca-
tion depth that allocates computational resources proportional to query 
complexity and domain-speciﬁc risks (Asai et al., 2023; Manakul et al., 
2023; Gao et al., 2023a).
9.2.4.  Open-source and privacy-preserving architectures
Proprietary LLM APIs limit the cost, privacy, and reproducibility of 
veriﬁcation systems considerably. Future research could systematically 
analyze the potential of open-source models such as LLaMA 2 (Touvron 
et al., 2023) as drop-in replacements in cross-model veriﬁcation systems, 
evaluating the performance-cost trade-oﬀ in these systems. Federated 
veriﬁcation approaches that localize entity-grounding and entailment 
scoring while preserving the conﬁdentiality of input queries is an im-
portant avenue for veriﬁcation within privacy-sensitive contexts.
9.2.5.  Multimodal hallucination veriﬁcation
The extension of veriﬁcation systems to multimodal content, includ-
ing images, audio, and structured data, is an inevitable necessity for 
the growing capabilities of generative models. Augmented hallucina-
tion learning for multimodal LLMs (Jiang et al., 2024) and multimodal 
factuality evaluation (Zhang et al., 2024b) establish important founda-
tions, but benchmark datasets and end-to-end veriﬁcation pipelines for 
multimodal claims remain severely underdeveloped in the existing lit-
erature.
9.2.6.  Alignment, ethics, and governance
Future research should focus on addressing the ethical implications 
of verifying the output of LLMs, including automation bias (Beauvais, 
2022; Adams et al., 2023), governance of veriﬁcation decisions in high-
stakes contexts (Jeong et al., 2025; Thorne, 2024), and aligning veriﬁ-
cation systems with diverse cultures and epistemic norms (Hendrycks 
et al., 2020; Perez et al., 2022). The research by  Perez et al. (2022) 
shows that even aligned models can generate hallucinations under ad-
versarial prompting conditions, establishing red teaming of veriﬁcation 
systems as an important part of the veriﬁcation process. The issue of 
veriﬁcation infrastructure governance which includes questions such as 
control over evidence sources and credibility tier assignment and cor-
rection decisions, is a neglected sociotechnical problem that requires 
interdisciplinary collaboration.
9.3.  Directions for strengthening this survey’s methodology
In addition to the open questions for future research inherent in the 
surveyed literature itself (Section 8), the review methodology on which 
this survey is based (Section 2) presents a number of avenues for im-
proving the robustness of any future iterations of this analysis. To start 
with, future updates to this survey could conduct a pre-registered sys-
tematic review against all included literature to address the publication 
bias whereby studies presenting novel or positive ﬁndings tend to be 
more readily published and indexed than studies presenting null or neg-
ative ﬁndings. Secondly, broadening the search protocol to venues out-
side the English language could help future studies take into account 
any veriﬁcation research not covered by the English-language literature 
considered in this survey. Next, given the rapid developments occurring 
in the ﬁeld of hallucination detection and veriﬁcation, one promising 
approach going forward is to create a living version of this survey by 
periodically updating the search protocol cut-oﬀ date (Section 2). Ad-
ditionally, future replication of the comparative analysis conducted in 
this survey (Section 1.2) could use an independently validated multi-
rater score as well as the structured author judgments conducted in this 
study. Finally, as discussed in Section 9.2, conducting the full empirical 
evaluation of the TruthGuard AI architecture proposed in this survey 
(Section 1) is a critical next step towards validating this concept.

This survey has presented a comprehensive review of hallucination 
detection, evaluation, mitigation, and veriﬁcation research in genera-
tive AI systems, synthesizing literature spanning 2016 through 2026

across eight major research dimensions. We established deﬁnitional 
clarity around hallucination types, healthcare, law, scientiﬁc research, 
education, and public information. We then reviewed state-of-the-art 
methodologies across retrieval-augmented generation, automated fact-
checking, natural language inference, language modeling, misinforma-
tion detection, cross-model veriﬁcation, and trustworthy AI frameworks. 
Key application domains, including healthcare, law, scientiﬁc research, 
education, and public information, were examined alongside their en-
abling technologies, such as dense retrieval, knowledge graphs, and 
credibility scoring. A comparative analysis revealed the superiority of 
convergent multi-signal architectures over single-layer approaches, a 
persistent gap between automated metrics and human evaluation, and 
substantial domain dependence. Several consistent lessons emerge. Hal-
lucination is a multi-dimensional phenomenon resisting single veriﬁca-
tion methodologies. Combining retrieval-based, cross-model, and log-
ical consistency signals provides the most robust detection. Enabling 
technologies are complementary rather than competitive, with each ad-
dressing distinct hallucination categories that other technologies can-
not cover alone. Furthermore, application domains determine neces-
sary precision–recall trade-oﬀs, demanding conﬁgurable architectures. 
Finally, open challenges like scalability, temporal inconsistency, inter-
pretability, and multilingual coverage deﬁne a critical research agenda. 
Systematic hallucination veriﬁcation remains a societal imperative, and 
the research agenda outlined in this survey provides a structured path-
way toward trustworthy, interpretable, and domain-adaptive generative 
AI systems.
