Course Goals

Learning Objectives

The course teaches how to conduct research and write strong research proposals — skills applicable beyond graduate study, including workplace problem-solving and organizational proposals.

After completing the course, you should be able to:

  • Identify, characterize, and state important problems in information quality and related fields
  • Formulate and justify research questions based on impact, knowledge gaps, theory, and prior work
  • Identify, synthesize, and critically evaluate relevant literature
  • Select appropriate research designs and methods
  • Write a research proposal with methods for collecting, analyzing, managing, and reporting data
  • Communicate research plans and results verbally, visually, and in writing
  • Formulate dissemination plans (conferences, journals, articles, blogs)
  • Reflect on professional development needs related to research design and reporting

Understanding the Research Process — due August 31, 2026 at 9:59 PM (PDT).

Core Concept

What Is Research?

Research is a systematic and methodical process of inquiry aimed at exploring, analyzing, and interpreting information to answer specific questions or solve problems.

Key ideas from common definitions:

  • Structured inquiry with organized steps for data collection, analysis, and interpretation
  • Objective, evidence-based reasoning rather than personal opinion
  • Empirical evidence from observation, experiments, or surveys
  • Contribution to an existing body of knowledge

Framework

Characteristics of Good Research

  • Systematic approach — organized, logical steps
  • Objectivity — conclusions based on evidence, not preference
  • Empirical evidence — grounded in observable data
  • Clear research question — drives objectives and methodology
  • Replicability — others can reproduce the study with similar results
  • Transparency and ethics — informed consent, confidentiality when humans are involved
  • Generalizability — findings may apply beyond the original sample
  • Critical and logical thinking — valid reasoning throughout
  • Novelty and impact — advances knowledge; often disseminated through peer review
  • Iterative process — results can open new questions and follow-on studies
  • Practical application — may inform policy, practice, or real-world problems

Methodologies

Types of Research

Quantitative vs. qualitative

  • Quantitative — measures phenomena numerically; examples include experiments, correlations, and statistical descriptions
  • Qualitative — explores experiences and social phenomena not easily quantified; examples include interviews, focus groups, and observation

Applied vs. basic

  • Applied — solves practical problems (e.g., medical image analysis, dynamic pricing, supply chain forecasting)
  • Basic — expands fundamental knowledge (e.g., universe origins, new ML optimization theory)

Other common types

  • Correlational — studies relationships between variables (not always causal)
  • Descriptive — profiles events or groups using surveys, case reports, or census-style analysis
  • Experimental (case-control) — tests cause-and-effect; strong for causality but can face feasibility limits
  • Exploratory — clarifies ill-defined problems before formal study design (clustering, anomaly detection)
  • Ethnographic — in-depth study of cultures and social groups
  • Mixed methods — combines quantitative and qualitative phases for richer insight

Class example: correlational research in practice

Professor Wu described a family science project measuring brain reaction time with and without music using a custom hardware instrument, refined across multiple years with t-tests — illustrating how research questions evolve as results emerge.

Topic Development

Formulating a Research Topic

  1. Find your focus — choose a field you enjoy and where you have relevant knowledge
  2. Review current literature — read recent journal and conference papers; check future work, limitations, and open questions
  3. Identify a gap — research must be unique and novel, not a replication of existing work
  4. Use the PIC framework — People (who benefits), Issues (core problem), Context (setting/environment)
  5. Test feasibility — data access, quality, trustworthiness, time limits, IRB if human subjects involved
  6. Assess interest — pick a topic that sustains your curiosity and matches your background
  7. Refine and get feedback — draft a clear research question and share with your advisor for approval

Deliverable

Research Proposal Key Components

  • Introduction / problem statement — define the research problem and literature gaps
  • Literature review — demonstrate state of the art; compare and contrast your approach with existing work
  • Methodology — data sources/access and analysis methods aligned with research questions
  • Expected outcomes — potential contributions and limitations
  • Timeline — recommended for dissertation proposals so committees understand scope
  • References — all sources cited in the proposal sections

Methodology is especially critical: it must justify how each component answers your research questions and explain whether the study is quantitative, qualitative, or mixed methods.

Q&A

Discussion Highlights

Realistic research timelines

Timelines depend on scope. Master projects typically allow about one year; dissertations up to about five years. Work with your advisor to right-size the topic — not too broad, not too narrow. Finalize timelines after you and your advisor agree on scope.

Real data vs. synthetic data

  • Prefer real data when accessible — results are more convincing
  • If synthetic data is necessary, use widely accepted datasets already used in the field
  • Do not create your own synthetic data — reviewers may distrust self-generated datasets as biased or tailored to desired outcomes

Literature Strategy

Finding Research Gaps

  • Start with a field of interest, then scan top journals and conferences for trends
  • Use Google Scholar with focused keywords; filter by engineering/computer science subcategories when relevant
  • Prioritize papers that are recent, published in top venues, and highly cited
  • Read future work, limitations, and open-question sections for gap identification
  • Discuss ideas with classmates, colleagues, and advisors to refine topics
