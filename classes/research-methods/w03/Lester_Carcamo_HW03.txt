Assigned paper: A systematic review on how educators teach AI in K-12 education

1. How many articles did the authors review for this paper?

The authors reviewed 45 papers. The abstract cites 45 high-quality empirical studies, which is the count remaining after database screening; 2 more were added through snowballing, bringing the final coding set to 47.

2. What corpus (collections, sources, or conference proceedings of papers) did they examine?

- Databases: Web of Science Core Collection and Scopus (PRISMA-guided search, conducted April 2024)
- Supplement: three rounds of backward and forward snowballing, seeded from well-cited literature reviews and already-selected papers
- Date range: January 2014 - April 2024, chosen because the term "AI literacy" was introduced in 2016
- Publication type: English-language journal articles only; conference papers excluded, and Scopus items had to be CiteScore Q1 or Q2 in Education
- Subject area: "Education Educational Research" in WOS or "Social Science" in Scopus
- Content criteria: empirical studies, conducted in K-12, with students as participants, about teaching and learning AI
- Excluded: reviews and theoretical papers, higher education or adult education, studies with teachers as participants, and papers that mention AI without implementation details
- Quality appraisal: papers scored against Gao et al. (2023) criteria; two scoring below 2 were dropped

The authors note as a limitation that restricting the search to WOS and Scopus produced a representative but not comprehensive sample.

3. What keywords did they use to find potential articles to include in their literature review?

The authors constructed a Boolean search string using three concept blocks joined by AND, with synonyms joined by OR inside each block (Section 2.1):

Topics = ("Artificial Intelligence" OR "Machine Learning" OR "Deep Learning" OR "Neuralnetwork*" OR "Natural Language Processing") AND (Education OR School OR Curriculum OR Curricula OR Course OR Learn* OR Teach* OR Instruction OR Pedagogy OR Student) AND ("K12" OR "K 12" OR "high school" OR "secondary school" OR "middle school" OR "primary school" OR "elementary school" OR Child* OR Kindergarten OR "Preschool")

The three blocks correspond to:

- AI technology terms, including specific subfields such as machine learning and natural language processing
- Education terms covering both institutions and instructional activity
- Grade-level terms spanning the full K-12 range from preschool through high school

Asterisks are truncation wildcards that capture word variants, so Learn* retrieves learn, learning, and learner, and Child* retrieves child and children. This search returned 20,738 Web of Science publications and 16,826 Scopus publications before any screening was applied.

4. Describe how the authors organized and presented their findings from their literature review.

Underneath the prose, the data is organized like a small relational database. The supplementary appendices are four linked tables:

- Appendix A identifies each paper. It has 45 rows, one per study, with a No column serving as the primary key, plus publication year, learning stage, educational setting, and the full citation.
- Appendix B extends Appendix A one-to-one on the same 45 keys, recording research design: sample size, duration, research method, research type, analysis methods, and research focus.
- Appendix C is a master-detail table. Each of the 45 papers is a parent row that expands into one or more child rows, one per measured learning effect. Attributes constant for a paper (pedagogical strategy, learning tool, assessment method) sit in merged cells, while attributes that vary by effect (domain, indicator, proved or unproved, p value, effect size) sit in the child rows.
- Appendix D is organized by theme rather than by paper. Its columns are Category, Sub-category, Frequency, Reference, and Description, with 90 rows across ten recommendation themes. A single paper can appear under several themes, so the relationship between papers and recommendations is many-to-many.

Overall, the organization is analytical rather than descriptive. The papers are treated as records in a dataset, and the prose reports what the dataset shows, which is the main way this review differs from the earlier reviews the authors criticize.

5. What were the major findings and opportunities for further research that the authors discovered from their literature review?

Findings:

The central finding is that K-12 AI education can develop students' AI literacy, which the authors define along three dimensions: 
    AI knowledge (concepts and techniques), 
    AI affectivity (perceptions of AI, humans, and society), and 
    AI thinking (higher-order thinking applied to unfamiliar problems).

Gaps:

1. Studies are too short and use too few students, so the results are hard to generalize.
2. Many studies were poorly designed. Of the 19 quasi-experiments, 14 had no control group, which makes it hard to say the teaching caused the learning.
3. Nobody is studying how to assess AI learning. Not one of the 45 papers focused on evaluation.
4. Lessons stay at the introductory level, and there is no long-term curriculum that builds across grades.
5. Students rarely work on real-world problems.
6. Teachers are not involved enough, and students get too little guidance and support.
