Defining Variables and Hypotheses in Research

Week 3 lecture with Professor Ningning Wu: how to define measurable variables in a study, why the relationships among variables matter, and when a research question is best expressed as a hypothesis.

Session Focus

Lecture Summary

The professor opened by clarifying that not every research proposal needs a formal hypothesis. Some research questions are descriptive or exploratory, while others can be translated into testable claims. For questions that can be framed as hypotheses, defining variables carefully is essential because the study cannot be analyzed well unless the key constructs are measurable and their relationships are explicit.

A strong research design starts by deciding what is being studied, which variables are included, and which ones are being manipulated or observed. The lecture used a plant-salt-tolerance example to show how variables become operational in practice: the amount of salt added is easily manipulated, while plant health and survival are outcomes that may need proxy measures such as growth and wilting status.

Measurement

Types of Variables

A variable is an attribute of the object under study. In research, the critical decision is not just choosing the variables but choosing the right ones to measure and the right way to represent them.

Quantitative vs. categorical

  • Quantitative variables represent amounts or measured values.
  • Discrete variables are count-based, such as the number of students, tree species, or incidents.
  • Continuous variables can take many values across a range, such as distance, time, speed, or volume.
  • Categorical variables represent groupings or labels.

Levels of categorical data

  • Binary — only two values, such as pass/fail or yes/no.
  • Nominal — categories with no inherent order, such as flower color or brand.
  • Ordinal — categories with a meaningful order, such as letter grades or Likert-type survey responses.

The lecture emphasized that the same attribute can be recorded in different ways depending on the research need. For example, age can be treated as a precise numeric value or grouped into age bands such as youth, middle age, or older adults. The choice depends on the purpose of the study and the practicalities of data collection.

Analysis

Variable Relationships

Researchers also need to describe the relationship among variables so that the design supports valid conclusions.

  • Independent variable — the variable the researcher can manipulate or vary.
  • Dependent variable — the outcome affected by the independent variable.
  • Control variable — held constant to reduce irrelevant variation.
  • Confounding variable — an outside factor affecting both the independent and dependent variables, potentially creating a misleading relationship.
  • Predictor variable — used in predictive or correlational analysis to explain or estimate an outcome.
  • Latent variable — not directly observed, but inferred from other observed indicators.
  • Composite variable — created during analysis by combining several variables into one measure.

The professor used the plant example to show how a study can distinguish between treatment levels and observed results. In that design, salt concentration is an independent variable while plant growth or survival is the dependent variable. If pot size or soil type differs across cases, those factors may become confounders unless controlled.

Demographic variables were also introduced as important attributes in human-subjects research. These may include age, gender, income, education, religion, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status. They are useful for grouping participants, describing sample composition, and analyzing whether outcomes differ across groups.

Methodology

Hypotheses and Testing

Hypothesis testing begins with an educated claim or assumption about a population parameter. In practice, the researcher forms two complementary statements:

  • Null hypothesis (H0) — the default or status-quo claim; it is usually the opposite of what the researcher wants to show.
  • Alternative hypothesis (H1 or Ha) — the researcher’s belief or claim.

In a statistical test, the goal is not to prove the alternative hypothesis absolutely but to evaluate whether the evidence is strong enough to reject the null hypothesis in favor of the alternative. This is a decision based on data, sampling variability, and the chosen significance threshold.

The lecture stressed that a hypothesis must be specific, testable, and connected to measurable variables. If the study is descriptive or exploratory, a formal hypothesis may not be necessary. The guiding principle is whether the research question can be transformed into a claim that can be checked empirically with data.

Action Items

  1. List the variables in your own topic and classify each as quantitative, categorical, independent, dependent, control, or confounding.
  2. Decide which measurements are directly observed and which are proxies or latent indicators.
  3. Write at least one research question and determine whether it should be framed as a hypothesis or left as a descriptive question.
  4. Identify how demographic variables might matter in a human-subject study.
  5. Prepare to explain why a null hypothesis and alternative hypothesis are defined as opposites in a statistical test.
