Week 2 Deliverable

Assignment: Introduce Your Research Topic

Open the Defining a Problem homework page

Write a single-page problem statement introducing a research topic you are interested in. Suggested structure:

  1. Hook — open with a compelling statistic, provocative question, or striking fact (the class example used identity theft and privacy protection: number of victims and dollar losses).
  2. Background and context — keep it tight; only the most important framing fits in one page.
  3. Problem statement and research questions — state the problem clearly and list the questions you would answer.
  4. Significance — what changes in the field if the research succeeds.
  5. Potential methodology — briefly, the approach that could answer the questions.

Going over one page is acceptable. Start the topic search early — the course ends with a full research proposal.

Topic Discovery

Where to Find a Research Problem

1. Workplace observation

Recurring problems you or your colleagues hit daily are legitimate research sources. Example from past doctoral students: building a shared development environment with version control, code sharing, and code validation so that duplicated code and untested changes stop breaking the path from development to production.

2. Literature gap analysis

Collect journal papers, articles, and credible blogs on your topic and analyze them for open problems. To find the papers that matter fastest:

  • Highly cited papers — usually published in top venues or by well-known authors
  • Top-tier journals and conferences — broader, more rigorous audience
  • Major researchers in the field — open problems they flag tend to be the significant ones
  • Recency matters — prioritize the most recent important work

Discovering that someone else works on the same problem concurrently is not fatal: different methodologies usually make both publishable.

Screening Criteria

Evaluating a Research Problem

  • Validated by literature — multiple sources treat it as critical and unsolved
  • Significant — solving it has real positive impact on the field
  • Timely — aligned with current trends and the present state of the field
  • Novel — the problem, or at least your methodology, is not a repeat of existing work
  • Clear objectives — it yields specific research goals you can act on
  • Researchable — you have the background knowledge, data, tools, labs, and time

If part of a problem has already been studied, ask what remains and whether that remainder is still significant and researchable. Significant and researchable are both mandatory — an important but non-researchable problem is not a viable topic.

Proposal Anatomy

Key Components of a Research Proposal

  • Introduction — defines the research problem and why it matters
  • Problem statement — identifies the gap in the literature
  • Research objectives and questions — specific and tied to the problem
  • Literature review — positions the study in the scholarly conversation
  • Methodology — data collection and analysis plan
  • Expected outcomes / significance — contribution to the field
  • Limitations — optional in a proposal; better understood once the work is nearly finished, and usually a dissertation chapter instead
  • Timeline — a tentative plan so the committee can judge scope

A timeline invites useful committee feedback: if the scope is too broad they help narrow it; if too narrow they help expand it to dissertation weight. Typical dissertation research runs roughly 2–3 years minimum, often 3–5.

Writing

Introduction & Problem Statement

The introduction sets the stage for the whole study — treat it like the abstract of a paper: simple, concise, clear.

Critical elements

  • State the problem — define it and explain why it matters
  • Contextualize — situate it in the academic landscape, referencing key studies
  • Outline the approach — a one-line roadmap (e.g., surveys plus machine learning), details saved for the methodology section

Do

  • Present the problem clearly and concisely
  • Justify relevance with background and context
  • Outline objectives and research questions
  • Identify the gap and link the problem to the larger field

Don't

  • Open with broad, vague statements
  • Overload the introduction with methodology detail
  • Ignore existing research — show what you build on

A strong problem statement is specific, researchable, and directly linked to the study objectives. A typical sample runs a few paragraphs: gaps in current work with citations, the research still needed, then one closing sentence on what the present study will do.

Alignment

Research Objectives & Questions

  • Align every question with the problem statement
  • Be precise; consult the scholarly literature to keep questions relevant and timely
  • Split a question that is too broad into sub-questions
  • Do not propose objectives that cannot realistically be achieved with your time and resources

Foundation

Literature Review Best Practices

The literature review establishes your knowledge of the field, builds the theoretical framework, and convinces the committee you know the state of the art. Effort here is reusable: most of the proposal review carries into the dissertation.

Four stages

  1. Search — databases, open-access journals, AI-assisted tools
  2. Identify — you will always have more papers than you need
  3. Select — keep the most relevant, important, high-quality work
  4. Synthesize — organize thematically, chronologically, or by methodology

Do

  • Synthesize and critically engage — trends, gaps, agreements, disagreements
  • Compare and contrast each relevant approach with your own; the differences are usually where your contribution lives
  • Report both contributions and limitations of each work
  • Address conflicting research rather than hiding it

Don't

  • Treat the review as a list of references or a string of summaries
  • Include work with no relevance to your problem

Narrowing a topic

Split a broad topic into progressively finer subtopics (e.g., software → programming languages → language classification → object-oriented languages; or software → operating systems → OS design → real-time / embedded systems). Too broad and you cannot focus; too narrow and it is a paper or master's project rather than a dissertation. Your advisor helps find the balance.

Design

Methodology & Data

  • Methods must align with the research questions and objectives, with justification for each choice
  • State whether the study is quantitative, qualitative, or mixed methods
  • Detail data collection (surveys, interviews, experiments), sampling strategy, and analysis techniques
  • Confirm feasibility: resources, time, skills, tools, and computing access
  • Address ethics — IRB approval and privacy protection whenever human participants or personal data are involved

Data sources, in order of preference

  1. Real-world data — best, but often hard to obtain
  2. Established synthetic datasets — acceptable when widely used and accepted in the field
  3. Data you generate yourself — strongly discouraged; results are hard to trust. If unavoidable, publish the dataset, document its statistical properties, and invite validation by other researchers

Naming limitations of your data or methods signals honesty and depth to the committee; it does not weaken the proposal.

Class Q&A

Survey Design Guidance

  • You can change or extend methods later — adding interviews after a survey is normal
  • Recruiting enough participants is the hardest part of survey research
  • Design carefully up front so one round covers what you need; expert and advisor review of the instrument is worth the time
  • Some studies run two rounds (pre- and post-implementation), e.g., before and after a data governance rollout
  • Incomplete responses are normally discarded — expect meaningful attrition from your raw response count
  • Aim for at least 20–30 usable responses; more is better, since small samples invite criticism about representativeness
  • Diversity matters — cover geographies or industries relevant to the population
  • Keep it short; roughly 15 minutes is a practical ceiling, especially for senior organizational participants

Formatting

Citation Styles

  • MLA — author name with page number in text; references alphabetized by first author's last name
  • Chicago — note/numeric citation with numbered references
  • APA — author last name and year in parentheses; references alphabetized. UALR dissertations generally follow APA
  • ACM — supports numeric citation or author-date in brackets
  • IEEE — numeric citation
  • Springer Nature — numeric (Vancouver: superscript numbers, references numbered in order of appearance) or author-date

Reference lists are ordered either alphabetically by first author or by order of first citation, depending on the publisher. Always follow the target venue's template.

Academic Integrity

Avoiding Plagiarism

Plagiarism is presenting another author's words, ideas, data, or structure as your own without proper citation — most cases are unintentional, from citations added late and then forgotten.

Types covered

  • Direct copying — sentences or paragraphs reused without citation
  • Patch writing — copied sentence with a few words swapped
  • Uncited paraphrase — your own wording, still no citation
  • Mosaic — short phrases from a source woven into your sentences
  • Self-plagiarism — reusing your own published text without citing yourself

Summarizing safely

  1. Read the full source, then write the summary from memory rather than from the page
  2. Identify the core claim and rebuild the sentence in your own words
  3. Change sentence order and structure, not just synonyms
  4. Compare your paraphrase side by side with the original for leftover phrasing
  5. Add the in-text citation — the single most important step

Detection tools such as Turnitin, Authenticate, and PaperPal are widely used and strict, so paraphrase carefully even when your work is original.

Tooling

Using AI Responsibly

  • AI is a strong assistant for locating and summarizing literature — AI-assisted databases such as Consensus and Elicit help surface and summarize papers
  • Running two different tools often returns different "most important" paper sets; combining them reduces the chance of missing key references
  • Validate everything — LLMs hallucinate, and every claim, statistic, and reference needs human verification
  • LLMs are non-deterministic: the same prompt returns different revisions each time, and later versions are not necessarily better — the model never signals when to stop
  • AI detection tools are unreliable in the other direction too; technical terminology and citations can trigger false "AI-generated" flags, so human review is required before acting on a flag
  • For this course's assignments, do not use AI to write your answers — the point is your own literature work and reasoning

Reference management: Zotero is free and handles collecting, organizing, annotating, citing, and sharing research, with grouping by topic or field.
