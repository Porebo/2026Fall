## 3.2 Research Design

### 3.2.1 Data Quality Dimension: Completeness

This design will focus on measuring completeness. Lee et al. (2006) described three kinds of completeness (schema completeness, column completeness, and population completeness), and the one that applies here is column completeness: "the degree to which there exist missing values in a column of a table" (p. 56). To apply the definition, each wellbore diagram will be treated as a record, and each data item that Section 1726.4.1 of Title 14 of the California Code of Regulations says a casing diagram "shall at a minimum include" (Casing Diagrams, 2026, subd. (a)(1)) will be treated as a required attribute of that record. In simple terms, in a table each wellbore diagram is a row, and the columns are the data required for the record to be complete. A diagram is complete when all of its columns have data, and it is incomplete when any column is missing data. Each diagram is an information product, and the artificial intelligence (AI) model will be asked to identify whether that information product is complete.

Completeness of a record will be measured as indicated by Lee et al. (2006, p. 56), as shown in Equation 1.

Completeness of a wellbore diagram = 1 − (number of required items that are missing ÷ number of required items that apply to the well)   (1)

The regulation lists 18 required data items, lettered (A) through (R), in subdivision (a)(1), and each of these items is one column in the table. All 18 items apply to every well. For example, a diagram that is missing 3 of them has a completeness of 1 − (3 ÷ 18) = 0.83, or 83%. A diagram that shows all 18 items has a completeness of 1, or 100%.

Measuring other data quality dimensions (accuracy, consistency, believability, appropriate amount of data, timeliness, and accessibility) is not possible in this study, because it would require checking the diagrams against the operators' internal records.

At the point of data collection, the data will be captured from CalGEM's public website, WellSTAR (CalGEM, n.d.). Before publication, the data go through a long information product line: each operator draws on its own internal records to supply the data the regulation requires, prepares the diagram, and submits it to CalGEM, which then makes it publicly available. Those internal records are private; access to them is not available for this study and will not be sought. The study will therefore not examine whether the values shown on a diagram are correct, current, or consistent with other records for the well. It will examine only whether each required item is shown and whether the AI model can detect its presence or absence.

### 3.2.2 Design Type and Variables

This study will use a within-subject experimental design. Charness et al. (2012) defined it as follows: "In a 'within-subject' designed experiment, each individual is exposed to more than one of the treatments being tested" (p. 1). In this study, the individual is a wellbore diagram, and the treatments are the four prompt types. Each wellbore diagram will be exposed to all four prompt types. The study is an experiment because the researcher will write the prompts and decide which prompt type the AI model receives.

Charness et al. (2012) named three advantages of a within-subject design: (a) internal validity that does not depend on random assignment, (b) often more statistical power, and (c) a closer match to how theory describes a subject reacting to a change (p. 2). They also named its disadvantages, which are confounds that can be introduced by exposing each subject to several treatments, such as the order of exposure and carry-over from one treatment to the next (pp. 2–3). According to the authors, causal estimates can be obtained "as long as there is independence of the multiple exposures" (p. 1), and researchers who use this design "need to make the case that the confounds discussed above do not pose a challenge for the results" (p. 7).

Figure 1 shows the variables of the study and how they relate. The independent variable, or treatment, will be the prompt type. It has four levels: naive, structured (persona, task, context, and format), few-shot, and chain-of-thought. The dependent variable will be the correctness of the AI model's judgments about the presence or absence of each required item. For each of the 18 required items, the AI model's answer will be scored as correctly judged, wrongly judged, or not addressed. Scoring will yield a count of correct judgments, out of 18, for each wellbore diagram under each prompt type. Two control variables will be held constant: the regulation and the wellbore diagram. The same regulation text will be given to the AI model in every run, where a run is one prompt type applied to one wellbore diagram. For any one wellbore diagram, exactly the same file will be given to the AI model with each of the four prompt types. The wellbore diagram is also the subject of the experiment.

The dependent variable will be scored with a measuring instrument, the gold standard, which is an answer key that records, for each wellbore diagram, whether each of the 18 required items is shown. The gold standard will never be given to the AI model, and the same gold standard will be used to score the AI model's answers under all four prompt types.

#### Implications of the Design

Choosing a within-subject design has implications for how the study will be carried out, for how its results will be analyzed, and for what the study must defend.

**Carrying out the study**

1. Every wellbore diagram will be tested under all four prompt types. If a run fails or its answer is lost, the run will be repeated, or the wellbore diagram will be removed from the analysis, so that every wellbore diagram in the analysis has four scores.
2. Each run will be independent of the others. Every run will start in a new chat, so that the AI model carries no information from one run to the next [confirm].

**Analyzing the results**

3. Each run will produce 18 item results. Each required item will be recorded as correctly judged, wrongly judged, or not addressed.
4. Each run will have one score, which is the count of correctly judged items, from 0 to 18. Items wrongly judged and items not addressed will be counted separately, so that the two can be told apart.
5. Each wellbore diagram will have four scores, one for each prompt type. The comparison will be made within each wellbore diagram, across its four scores.
6. The scores will be analyzed as related measurements. The four scores for one wellbore diagram come from the same subject, so statistical tests for related samples will be used, and tests for independent groups will not (see Section 3.3).
7. Each wellbore diagram will serve as its own comparison. A wellbore diagram that is hard to read will be equally hard to read under all four prompt types, so differences among wellbore diagrams will not be mistaken for differences among prompt types.

**Defending the design**

8. The study must make the case that the disadvantages named by Charness et al. (2012) do not affect its results. That case rests on two points made above: the subject is a document that cannot learn or change between treatments, and every run will start in a new chat. Section 3.6 returns to these risks.

The alternative, a between-subject design, would give each wellbore diagram only one prompt type. Charness et al. (2012) described between-subject designs as more conservative but as having less statistical power than within-subject designs (p. 7).

### 3.2.3 Sample Selection

The sample was selected on November 11, 2026, under a sampling plan that was fixed before any well was screened. The target population was unplugged California oil and gas wells, with a status of active or idle, that have at least one wellbore diagram on file in WellSTAR. The sampling frame was CalGEM's 2021 file of California oil and gas wells, which lists 107,952 distinct wells with a status of active or idle [source needed]. The frame does not show which wells have a wellbore diagram on file, so each well had to be screened in WellSTAR.

The sample was a simple random sample without replacement. A script with a fixed seed placed the frame in a random order, and the wells were screened strictly in that order. A well was included when it met four rules: (a) it was found in WellSTAR, (b) it was not plugged, (c) it had at least one wellbore diagram showing the existing condition of the well, and (d) the file could be downloaded and was legible to a person. No well was chosen or skipped by judgment. Screening stopped at the 30th well that met the rules. In all, 218 wells were screened: 30 were included, 43 had been plugged since 2021, and 145 had no wellbore diagram on file. Among the 175 wells that were still unplugged, 30 (17.1%) had a wellbore diagram on file, 95% CI [11.6%, 22.7%].

The sample size of 30 wellbore diagrams was set as the size of a pilot study. Lancaster et al. (2004) stated that "a general rule of thumb is to take 30 patients or greater to estimate a parameter" (p. 308). Hertzog (2008) evaluated pilot samples of 10 to 40 by the precision of the confidence intervals they yield (p. 180). By that method, a proportion near 0.50 estimated from 30 wellbore diagrams has a 95% confidence interval of about plus or minus 0.18, and a proportion near 0.90 has an interval of about plus or minus 0.11. Hertzog did not recommend a specific number for feasibility aims (p. 190), so the number 30 rests on Lancaster et al. (2004). With four prompt types, 30 wellbore diagrams will give 120 runs.

Three alternatives were considered. The first was a smaller sample. Julious (2005) recommended "a sample size of 12 per group" for pilot studies (p. 287); the sample of 30 exceeds that minimum and gives narrower confidence intervals. The second was a sample size calculated for a full study. That calculation needs an estimate of how often the AI model judges an item correctly, and no such estimate exists yet. The pilot study will supply it, and the size of any full study will then be set with Cochran's formula and the finite population correction [source needed]. The third was to exclude or replace wellbore diagrams whose files are of poor quality, such as low-resolution scans. None was excluded or replaced. Removing a file after it was randomly selected would end the random sample, and it would favor the AI model by leaving the files that are easiest to read.

The 30 wellbore diagrams are identified as W01 to W30 and are used exactly as downloaded. Of the 30 files, 24 carry a text layer, 3 are mixed, and 3 are scanned images. The full sampling plan, the screening log, and the evaluation of file quality are in Appendix A [confirm].

## 3.3 Data Analysis

Because the four scores for one wellbore diagram come from the same subject, they are related measurements, and statistical tests for independent groups do not apply. The Friedman test will be used to test whether correctness differs across the four prompt types. If it does, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests will compare the prompt types in pairs, with the Holm correction for multiple comparisons [source needed]. These tests were chosen because the score is a count out of 18, and they do not assume that the scores follow a normal distribution.

## 3.6 Limitations and Risks

> Ideas from 3.2.1 to repeat here (working list, not proposal text):
>
> - **One dimension only.** The study measures completeness. It does not measure accuracy, consistency, believability, appropriate amount of data, timeliness, or accessibility.
> - **One kind of completeness only.** The study uses column completeness. Schema completeness and population completeness are not examined.
> - **No access to private records.** The operators' internal records are private; access is not available for this study and will not be sought.
> - **Values are not validated.** The study will not examine whether the values shown on a diagram are correct, current, or consistent with other records for the well. An item that is shown counts as present even if its value is wrong.
> - **The operator is not audited.** The study examines the published diagram, not the operator's records or practices.
> - **Only the published product is seen.** The data come from the public website, WellSTAR, at the end of a long information product line (operator's records, preparing the diagram, submission to CalGEM, publication). The earlier steps are not observed.
> - **Completeness is measured against 18 items.** The count covers items (A) through (R) of subdivision (a)(1) of the regulation, and all 18 are treated as applying to every well.
> - **Presence or absence only.** The AI model is asked to detect whether each required item is shown, nothing more.
