Effective research mentoring relationships form the foundation of a robust scientific workforce. In their most effective form, research mentoring relationships are reciprocal, evolve, support individuals’ personal and professional growth and success, and foster research productivity and discovery (NASEM 2019). Students are more likely to persist in their academic paths if engaged in positive mentoring experiences (McGee and Keller 2007; Williams et al. 2016). For example, when individuals from historically underrepresented groups have positive mentoring relationships they experience a greater sense of belonging in academic communities (Byars-Winston et al. 2015; Estrada et al. 2018; Lisberg and Woods 2018). Academic programs that provide high-quality mentoring have been found to positively influence the recruitment and retention rates of mentees (Pfund et al. 2016).

Like any relationship, mentoring relationships require purposeful strategies and practices to support maximal effectiveness and satisfaction. They also require constructive conflict management. This module discusses mentoring and why it is essential to research, factors that can make mentoring relationships challenging, and practices and strategies mentors and mentees can use to establish and navigate mentor-mentee relationships successfully. The module also describes a psychologically healthy and supportive research environment and the leadership behaviors mentors can use to create positive research environments.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

Explain the roles and responsibilities of mentors and mentees.
Identify challenges that can disrupt or undermine a mentoring relationship.
Describe practices and strategies that support high-quality mentoring relationships and help prevent or manage conflicts between mentors and mentees.
Describe leadership behaviors that cultivate a psychologically healthy and supportive work environment.
Overview of Mentoring
The traditional form of mentoring in research is when experienced researchers train and guide less experienced research mentees, such as undergraduate or graduate students, postdoctoral researchers, or junior faculty. Mentors share knowledge and skills with mentees, and they provide psychosocial support and career planning advice (NASEM 2019; Pfund et al. 2016). Mentors can also adopt the similar, yet distinct, roles of a coach or sponsor (Baker et al. 2021). Coaches ask thoughtful questions and provide impartial feedback to support personal and professional growth, while sponsors leverage their reputation to expose others to professional opportunities (Parsons and O’Connor 2023). For example, mentors can connect mentees to scientific and networking opportunities that could advance their research or careers. Mentors are often more than just scientific advisors or supervisors. Mentors are teachers, guides, coaches, supporters, and advocates for their mentees. A defining feature of a mentor is a sincere interest in a mentee's growth and professional development.

Mentoring in research also involves experienced mentees mentoring less experienced mentees. For example, a postdoctoral fellow or a fourth-year graduate student might mentor a first-year graduate student. Peers can also be mentors (Cree-Green et al. 2020; Dickson et al. 2021; Kuhn and Castaño 2016). Undergraduate students, graduate students, postdoctoral researchers, and faculty at all levels have guidance and support to offer peers at their same career stage. Peers in the same organization can provide valuable insights into how the organization functions. Additionally, many peers face, or have faced, similar situations and can provide support and brainstorm possible solutions. Some researchers identify their relationships with trusted confidants as the most important factor in their career success (Antes and DuBois 2018).

Pro Tip
            Researchers should seek mentoring from multiple individuals throughout their careers, as each mentor offers different strengths and perspectives. Consider creating a mentoring map that identifies areas where you would benefit from mentorship and multiple individuals who could provide mentoring in these areas. 

Most researchers play the role of both mentor and mentee in their careers. Sometimes researchers have both roles simultaneously. For example, a postdoctoral researcher might mentor graduate students and be a mentee of a principal investigator.

Practical Application: Moment of Reflection
            Are you a mentor, mentee, or both? Who might informally view you as a mentor in your research environment, even if you are not formally their mentor? Do you have trusted peers you consult for guidance and advice? If not, how might you identify and build social ties with peers?

Benefits of High-Quality Mentoring Relationships
The rigorous and multifaceted demands of scientific research make mentoring essential. Both mentors and mentees benefit from high-quality mentoring relationships (Antes and Maggi Jr. 2021; Gardiner et al. 2007; Heemstra and Garg 2022; NASEM 2019).

Benefits for Mentors

Mentors with reputations for exceptional mentoring attract mentees.
Mentees contribute new ideas and perspectives.
Mentees bring energy, enthusiasm, and hard work.
Mentors get satisfaction from sharing their expertise and observing mentees grow and succeed.
Mentors collaborate and coauthor with mentees.
Mentors develop their communication and leadership skills.
Mentors contribute to their scientific discipline and expand their professional network.
Benefits for Mentees

Mentees gain scientific knowledge and skills.
Mentees learn the cultures and norms of organizations and the broader scientific enterprise.
Mentees develop an understanding of research integrity and researchers' ethical, social, and professional responsibilities.
Mentees get career planning advice and access to a professional network.
Mentees receive support and encouragement.
Mentees are more productive and satisfied and persist in their scientific training.
Mentees collaborate and coauthor with mentors.
Mentees often build career-long collaborations and friendships in their training labs.

Expectations, Feedback, Guidance, and Training
Setting and aligning expectations is essential to the success of a mentoring relationship (Huskins et al. 2011; Masters and Kreeger 2017; Norris et al. 2018). Mentors and mentees need to have a mutual understanding of their expectations for each other. Mentors need to be available to mentees, and regular meetings are important to make progress on research (Antes et al. 2019b; Cho et al. 2011). Feedback and guidance should be routine and constructive, allowing the mentee to gain greater independence over time. Mentors should also ensure mentees receive the scientific training they need to develop their skills and perform research competently, responsibly, and ethically.

Psychosocial Support
Mentors provide mentees with psychosocial support, which includes psychological and emotional support; encouragement; help with problem-solving; and role modeling of values, attitudes, and behaviors (NASEM 2019). Research involves inevitable setbacks and failures, and mentees need to understand that failure can lead to growth (Van Bavel et al. 2019). Mentors should also communicate about and create laboratory policies and share institutional resources that support the well-being of mentees. In addition, mentors should provide opportunities to celebrate mentees' successes (Antes et al. 2019a; Limas et al. 2022; Maestre 2019; Masters and Kreeger 2017).

Playing an Active Role in the Mentoring Relationship
While mentors guide and support mentees, mentees are responsible for playing an active role in their own research and success (Lakoski 2019). Mentees should take ownership of their role in the mentoring relationship by being self-motivated, solving problems independently when they can, asking for what they need from their mentor, and seeking outside mentoring or support when necessary (Antes and Maggi Jr. 2021; Zerzan et al. 2009). One mentor cannot meet all the needs of a mentee, so mentees should develop robust mentoring networks with individuals from their own organization and externally (Montgomery and Page 2018).

Psychologically Healthy and Supportive Training Environment
Excellent mentors cultivate and maintain a healthy and supportive training environment, and mentees should also contribute positively to this environment (Antes et al. 2019a; Antes and Maggi Jr. 2021; Antes 2022). A healthy and supportive environment is one where everyone feels respected, has access to resources and information to be successful, and is empowered to speak up and play an active role in their career development without shame or fear of retaliation (Carmeli and Hoffer Gittell 2009; Edmondson and Lei 2014; Hauff et al. 2022; Hirak et al. 2012; Kim et al. 2021; Gube and Hennelly 2022). Treating members of research teams with respect is as essential to research ethics as the responsibility to produce trustworthy data or protect human subjects (DuBois and Antes 2018; Antes et al. 2019a).

The Importance of Mentoring in Science
When mentoring is effective, the outcomes are generally positive for mentees, mentors, research teams, research organizations, and the scientific enterprise, including the public and community stakeholders.

Mentoring plays a critical role in the future of research in multiple ways:

Shaping the next generation of scientists
            • Enhancing retention and broadening participation in science
            • Improving the quality of scientific processes and findings

Shaping the Next Generation of Scientists
Mentoring strongly influences the next generation of scientists' behaviors, practices, and attitudes. Well-mentored mentees often become good mentors. Mentors are role models. They can help mentees identify and navigate ethical issues, understand the role of science in society, and develop the interpersonal skills they will need to be successful. Mentors can also convey knowledge of unwritten rules and nuanced guidelines, especially those related to the responsible and ethical conduct of research, that are not easily learned from books, written policies, or classroom instruction (Anderson et al. 2007; Bird 2001; Plemmons et al. 2020; Ripley et al. 2012; Titus and Ballou 2014).

Enhancing Retention and Broadening Participation in Science
Quality mentoring supports the retention of all mentees and the creation of a welcoming and nurturing environment (NASEM 2019). Excellent mentoring involves examining one’s own beliefs and acknowledging differences in the beliefs of others while accepting others for who they are (Yeager and Bauer-Wu 2013). Great mentors encourage and support underrepresented future researchers (Hinton Jr. et al. 2020; Murray et al. 2022; Powell 2018; Shuler et al. 2021). Mentors support mentees’ job satisfaction and productivity, which contribute to their retention and long-term career success (Detsky and Baerlocher 2007; Ehrich et al. 2004; Sambunjak et al. 2006). There are many setbacks and struggles in science, so tailored guidance and support are necessary. Excellent mentoring is essential to create a robust scientific workforce. Researchers, especially laboratory leaders, must be willing to discuss tough topics like intolerance and unfair power structures in science (McGee 2016; Mehta 2018).

Improving the Quality of Scientific Processes and Findings
Quality mentoring involves open communication, routine feedback, and training to foster scientific competence and independence. Thus, the rigor, reproducibility, and transparency of scientific data and findings rely on high-quality mentoring and training (Antes et al. 2019b; Knudtson et al. 2019; Rockey 2014). Mentors are the primary source by which mentees learn how to conduct reliable and meticulous research; manage, process, and share data; and report findings. This learning supports the transparency and trustworthiness of scientific data and findings, which mentees propagate as they advance in their careers and become mentors.

Challenges in Mentor-Mentee Relationships
The remainder of this module explores practices and strategies mentors and mentees can adopt to enhance their mentoring relationships. This module also covers leadership behaviors that create psychologically healthy and supportive environments. First, let us explore key challenges in mentoring relationships.

No mentoring relationship is perfect. Mentors and mentees are human beings. Time and energy are finite resources, and researchers typically manage many obligations. Challenges related to the relationship or tasks associated with a research project will inevitably emerge. Real-world dilemmas arise for mentors, such as the need for consistent productivity while being considerate of mentees’ needs and well-being (Resnik et al. 2023). Conflict is a foreseeable component of any relationship and must be managed effectively and respectfully to maintain the quality of the mentoring relationship. Environmental and situational factors can also present challenges in a mentoring relationship. The following table highlights some relationship, task-related, and environmental and situational challenges in mentoring:

Practices and Strategies to Enhance Mentoring Relationships
We have covered the roles and responsibilities of mentors and mentees and challenges that may arise in mentoring relationships. Mentors and mentees should enter mentoring relationships with an awareness of their responsibilities and potential challenges. Mentors and mentees should also commit to actively employing practices and strategies that help them effectively perform their responsibilities and navigate challenges (Antes et al. 2019a; Antes et al. 2019b; Pfund et al. 2006; NASEM 2019). It is helpful to conceptualize practices and strategies for well-functioning dynamics in research within two domains (McIntosh et al. 2020):

• Those focused on relationships 
            • Those focused on task-related activities 

The practical tips in this section apply to both mentors and mentees. We also provide a brief list of additional strategies for mentees.

Pro Tip
            Investing the time to develop and maintain quality relationships provides a foundation for working well together. 

Relationship-Building Practices and Strategies
These practices and strategies help establish a mutually supportive, respectful, open, and trusting relationship. The strategies focus on strengthening a social bond and developing mutual compassion and understanding. They can also help reduce and resolve conflict.

Relationship-Building Practices and Strategies

Keep an open mind and listen with the goal of understanding the other person. Use active listening techniques to facilitate quality communication and mutual understanding (Abrahams and Groysberg 2021; Bryant and Sharer 2021; Minehart et al. 2022; Zenger and Folkman 2016).
Approach conflict with curiosity and inquiry, not judgment.
Use non-defensive communication to explain your needs and boundaries assertively while also respecting the needs and boundaries of the other person (Ellison 2016; Rosenberg 2015; Ford 2002).
Get to know the person. For example, learn what they enjoy outside of research, their strengths and areas for growth, and their career goals.
Acknowledge that people have lives and an identity outside of their jobs. For example, recognize that the person may have interests or commitments in addition to their work; appreciate backgrounds, cultures, and worldviews that differ from yours; and ask people how they are doing.
Celebrate successes.
Express support when failures or setbacks occur.
Recognize that your perspective is limited. Invite others to share their perspectives and listen with curiosity.
Challenge your default assumptions and split-second judgments.
Avoid interacting when you are frustrated or when other negative emotions might affect your judgment (Seppälä and Bradley 2019).
Seek additional resources, expertise, or support related to your research, mentoring relationship, and/or well-being.

Task-Related Practices and Strategies
These practices and strategies help clarify and align expectations about project goals and plans. They also develop scientific knowledge and skills.

Task-Related Practices and Strategies

Set, align, and clarify expectations up front, and realign expectations over time. Consider jointly creating a written mentoring compact that establishes mutually agreed-upon expectations for the mentoring relationship, especially at the beginning of a mentoring relationship (White-Lewis et al. 2022).
Communicate regularly and establish routine meetings to discuss problems and progress toward milestones.
Arrive to meetings prepared and ask questions. This is particularly important for mentees as mentors may not know where mentees need guidance. Preparation may include taking a few minutes to ground yourself before the meeting to ensure you are fully present.
Communicate negative feedback constructively and provide positive feedback regularly. Two-way feedback, where mentors and mentees give and receive feedback, is ideal (Indeed Editorial Team 2023; Hirsch 2020; Miles 2022).
Speak up, advocate for yourself, and ask for clarification when there seem to be misaligned expectations or misunderstandings. Remain calm and open to feedback. This is especially important for mentees and early-career mentors.
Assess your mutual understanding of the next steps before implementing them.
Use time management and organization practices, and refine your approach over time. For example, be honest with yourself about how you spend your time and be realistic about what you can accomplish in a given amount of time.
Take time every day to manage stress so you can maintain your energy and well-being.
Especially for mentors, share stories of setbacks to normalize failure and rejection in science.
Seek out and support opportunities for professional development in research mentoring skills.

Additional Strategies for Mentees
Because of the power dynamics involved in mentoring relationships and the pressures mentees may experience, mentees should also consider additional strategies for success.

Additional Strategies for Mentees

Make a genuine effort to solve a problem or answer a question independently before approaching your mentor for help. This is not to say you should not ask your mentor for help. Rather, doing some initial troubleshooting on your own can help conversations with your mentor be more focused and productive.
Ask for clarification and guidance on new projects or tasks.
Ask for input on which tasks to prioritize, and proactively discuss and reestablish deadlines when necessary.
Engage in positive self-talk, which affects your thoughts, feelings, and self-efficacy. For example, instead of thinking, “I failed,” reframe your thinking to “I learned a lot and am proud of myself for trying.”
Take ownership of your success and recognize that a single mentor is unlikely to be able to meet all your needs.
Seek guidance and mentoring from multiple people, including peers.
Conflict Management
Most relationships involve some conflict, and mentoring relationships are no different. Conflict occurs when people disagree over opposing ideas, interests, or needs. Healthy conflict resolution centers on the problem, situation, or challenge rather than the persons involved and requires mutual problem-solving to identify resolutions (Capozzoli 2018; Krishnakumar et al. 2019).

Mentors and mentees can often resolve conflict amicably and repair, or even enhance, the relationship. Doing so requires holding a proactive and potentially difficult conversation. People tend to avoid conflict and difficult conversations to avoid negative emotions or outcomes. Avoidance often creates more problems than addressing conflict directly.

Sometimes, ending the mentoring relationship and moving on may be appropriate. People often struggle with this decision. Mentors and mentees typically want to keep their commitments to each other and prefer to avoid the mutual disruption that can occur when mentees must find a different mentor. People want to feel they diligently tried to address the problem before they choose to move on. In such situations, mentors and mentees must take initiative and invest emotional energy to discuss potential resolutions and paths forward. A conversation between the mentor and mentee is necessary to understand how both parties view the relationship and the needs and expectations surrounding the relationship (Antes and Maggi Jr. 2021).

Pro Tip
            It is natural to want to avoid challenging conversations, but often the long-term disruption caused by not having them exceeds the discomfort of having the conversation early on.

Tips for Critical Conversations
Before having a conversation, mentees and mentors should reflect on and test their assumptions about the relationship and conflict. They can ask themselves questions such as the following:

• What are the shared interests and goals of both partners? Am I making sure to avoid a defensive mindset of 'I am right, they are wrong'?
          • What is the source of conflict in the relationship from my perspective? How might the other person see it?
          • What did both partners agree to when establishing the relationship? Where are we falling short of our mutual commitments? 
          • What assumptions am I making about the other person? How can I set these aside? Am I giving them the benefit of the doubt until there is evidence I should not?
          • What is the root cause of what is upsetting or frustrating me? How are my emotions influencing my perception of the situation and my attitude toward the conversation?
          • What needs to change in the relationship to move forward constructively? How likely are these changes to resolve the problem and prevent its reoccurrence? How might the other person see my proposed changes? What changes might they propose? 

When in doubt, mentees and mentors should speak with someone with an outside perspective for advice and find out what resources their organization has that can help. Mentees and mentors should seek help from those with experience in mentoring and navigating challenges that emerge in mentoring relationships, such as another mentor, a peer or colleague, an institutional leader such as a program director or department chair, or someone from the organization’s ombuds office. The ombuds office is where people can informally and confidentially discuss concerns or issues and explore ways to respond to and resolve these issues. At times, it may be worthwhile to bring in an objective third party to help mediate a disagreement between a mentor and mentee.

If the conflict remains unresolved after exhausting the aforementioned approaches, options for moving forward include accepting and coping with the relationship as it is or leaving the relationship. Once the mentor and mentee have communicated, they should plan to reevaluate and follow up to determine if the relationship is improving or if the source of conflict persists. If both partners agree that the relationship is worth repairing, yet they are still struggling, ongoing conversations will be necessary.

Practical Application: Moment of Reflection

Discrimination, Harassment, and Bullying
Relationships naturally involve conflict, but bullying, harassment, and discrimination are unacceptable and should not be tolerated. Other toxic mentoring behaviors, such as neglecting the relationship, are also problematic and can negatively affect mentees in the long term.

In 2022, the National Institutes of Health (NIH) codified its priority of safe, healthy research environments that “promote inclusion and are free of sexual, racial, ethnic, disability and other forms of discriminatory treatment” (NIH 2022a). Furthermore, the NIH requires organizations to report when NIH-funded investigators are found to have created hostile working conditions or committed harassment, bullying, or retaliation (NIH 2022b). It is worth noting that nonretaliation clauses and statutes do not necessarily prevent retaliation but give victims of retaliation a legal basis for recovery.

As another example of prioritizing safe and healthy research environments and mentoring relationships, in 2023, the National Science Foundation (NSF) expanded the requirement for training in the responsible and ethical conduct of research to include faculty and other senior personnel, and this training must cover mentor training and mentorship (NSF n.d.).

Discrimination is the less favorable treatment of an individual based on “race, color, national origin, religion, sex (including pregnancy and gender identity), age, marital and parental status, disability, sexual orientation, or genetic information” (U.S. Department of the Interior n.d.).

Harassment is unwelcome or offensive verbal or physical conduct based on race, color, national origin, religion, sex (including pregnancy and gender identity), older age (beginning at 40), marital and parental status, disability, sexual orientation, or genetic information (U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission n.d.). These behaviors are unlawful when “1) enduring the offensive conduct becomes a condition of continued employment, or 2) the conduct is severe or pervasive enough to create a work environment that a reasonable person would consider intimidating, hostile, or abusive” (U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission n.d.). Federal law also prohibits retaliation against an individual participating in any way in an investigation (U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission n.d.). In addition to these protections under federal law, many states have laws that allow an employee to bring a civil action against employers when harassment has occurred.

Bullying occurs when there is repeated abusive treatment of a person, including threats, intimidation, humiliation, work sabotage, and verbal abuse (Wilkie 2016; Else 2018).

Mentors, mentees, and institutional staff are often mandatory reporters if they learn of or witness bullying or harassment. Check your organization’s policies to make sure you comply with the requirements.

If you find yourself in a situation where you are being bullied or harassed, seek help from institutional resources, such as a reporting hotline, and trusted people at your organization, such as a program director or ombudsperson. It might be intimidating to seek help, but support can help you navigate these circumstances.

Psychologically Healthy Work Environments
In addition to supporting individuals’ career development, responsible mentors cultivate healthy team environments, often referred to as “safe spaces” or, increasingly, “brave spaces” (Arao and Clemens 2013). It is incumbent on scientific mentors to create work environments where all team members from both historically over- and under-represented groups can thrive. For all team members to thrive, these spaces need to be open for critical conversations and feedback about aspects of sociocultural identity. Mentors should be aware of the power dynamics within the team and give and receive feedback in a respectful and humanistic way.

Scientific mentors are leaders who have an ethical responsibility for how they treat mentees and for the behaviors they model to others (Johnson 2020). A psychologically healthy and supportive work environment encourages members’ well-being and engagement. Members of such an environment exhibit mutual respect; are comfortable sharing their views regardless of their position or role; cooperate to complete tasks; and are willing to disclose mistakes or concerns without fear of retaliation, bullying, harassment, discrimination, or abuse (Hauff et al. 2022; Kim et al. 2021; Edmondson 2018; Edmondson and Lei 2014; Edmondson 2004; Ma et al. 2020). Psychologically healthy and supportive work environments also foster learning, productivity, rigorous science, and creativity (Antes et al. 2019b; Carmeli et al. 2010; Carmeli and Hoffer Gittell 2009; Hirak et al. 2012; Edmondson 1999; Edmondson 2003; Gube and Hennelly 2022). In these environments, members do the following:

- Speak up and feel comfortable doing so
    - Provide, receive, and respond to feedback without fear, personal attacks, or defensiveness
    - Believe that others value them and their input
    - Feel that decisions are generally fair
    - Trust one another and enjoy mutual respect
    - Feel they belong

One of the most important things a mentor can do to support positive relationships with and among their mentees is intentionally cultivating the research environment (Antes et al. 2019a). In a healthy work environment, leaders of research teams skillfully and consistently model behaviors that create this type of environment and encourage positive team dynamics. Of course, all research team members contribute to the research environment and are responsible for the respectful and equitable treatment of team members (Antes and Maggi Jr. 2021). Pressure and competition are inherent in many research settings, and a psychologically healthy environment can help the team navigate these challenges while continuing to support collaboration and mutual goals (Antes et al. 2019a; Peterson and Behfar 2022; Bradley et al. 2012).

Leadership Behaviors that Create and Maintain a Healthy and Supportive Research Environment
Creating a psychologically healthy and supportive environment takes intention, and leaders must monitor and maintain the environment over time. The following table highlights several leadership behaviors that help foster positive research environments (Antes 2018; Antes 2022; Bresman and Edmondson 2022; Byars-Winston and Butz 2021; Clark 2022; Clark 2023; Delizonna 2017; Edmondson and Hugander 2021; Edmondson and Mortensen 2021; Hauff et al. 2022; Maestre 2019; Mehta 2018):

Behavior

Description

Know the qualities you want to define your environment.

Mentors need to know what values they want to define their environment. They should explicitly convey these values when recruiting lab members, on the lab website, and in communications with lab members. Consider the following questions: What qualities do you want to characterize your environment? What actions will you take to demonstrate a healthy and supportive environment? How will you know if lab members feel supported? What could be done better or differently to foster a positive experience for lab members?

Take the time to build relationships with all members.

Mentors should make time to build one-on-one relationships with each lab member. This provides the opportunity to connect with people as individuals. Mentors should ask open-ended questions about how lab members are doing professionally and personally.

Model humility and ongoing learning.

Mentors need to model humility and ongoing learning. Examples of this include recognizing how research experiences might differ among lab members and adjusting interactions with lab members when they recognize or hear from others that their words or actions come across as insulting, insensitive, or dismissive.

Lead by example.

Leading by example in group settings is an effective way for mentors to create a positive team dynamic. Doing so sets clear expectations for how lab members should treat each other. Leaders must “walk the talk” and engage in behaviors they want others to adopt. Some examples of leading by example include encouraging open communication, framing differences in perspective as valuable, inviting input, asking for help, listening carefully, and admitting to gaps in knowledge.

Reinforce behaviors that align with the desired values for the work environment.

Mentors should acknowledge lab members who engage in behaviors that align with the values they want to characterize the work environment. Telling members what they did well communicates what the mentor values and reinforces behavior that supports the desired work environment.

Monitor the environment.

Mentors should regularly monitor the environment to ensure it is not being undermined or disrupted. They can do this by asking lab members in one-on-one conversations about life in the lab. If the lab is large enough, leaders can administer an anonymous survey that asks how lab members experience the lab environment. These activities can inform mentors of issues they may not have been aware of that they need to address.

Address behaviors that do not align with the values set for the environment.

Leaders must quickly address destructive and problematic behaviors. Leaders can do this by first encouraging people to address their own conflicts, which can help develop conflict resolution skills. Leaders should explicitly communicate expectations about behavior during lab meetings and through other modes of communication, such as email or in a lab manual. In some cases, it is appropriate to ask lab members to develop and share a plan for contributing positively to the environment, and leaders should hold them accountable to this plan.

Acknowledge the difficult, human part of research.

Research involves learning, failure, rejection, setbacks, mistakes, strong emotions, and stress. When mentors openly talk about their research challenges, mentees begin to understand that these experiences are normal and can be overcome.

Institutional Support of Responsible Mentoring and Leadership
Funding agencies emphasize responsible mentoring and healthy research environments because of their effect on the conduct of science and the development of future scientists (NIH 2021; NIH 2022a; NSF n.d.). Some grant applications may require and be scored on plans for responsible mentoring. Organizations can help reinforce quality mentoring and responsible leadership practices (Pizzolato et al. 2022). Organizations can do this by creating mentoring and leadership policies and guidelines, communicating an organization-wide vision of and commitment to excellent mentoring, providing training and resources needed to mentor well, incentivizing and rewarding good mentoring, taking steps to improve or remediate behaviors of bad mentors, and establishing and consistently adhering to procedures that protect whistleblowers (NASEM 2019).

Mentoring Training
The skills needed to be a good mentor and create a positive environment are not necessarily the same skills needed to conduct research (Clayton 2015). In the past, few scientists received training on how to mentor others. Regardless of their career stage, mentors may seek evidence-based mentorship education to develop requisite intra- and inter-personal skills (Pfund et al. 2014; Antes and DuBois 2018). Organizations should support these efforts by sponsoring participation (Pfund et al. 2006). Some programs provide training on how to be an effective mentee (Branchaw et al. 2020). Making quality mentor training accessible to scholars creates a unified institutional approach to supporting quality mentoring and cultivating a positive work environment at all levels of the organization (Pfund et al. 2006). Moreover, incentivizing participation in such training and rewarding mentoring excellence alongside traditional markers of research career success are critical for creating psychologically healthy work environments.

Further Advice for Mentors and Mentees on Fostering Excellent Mentoring Relationships and Psychologically Healthy Work Environments
Both mentors and mentees should consider several other practical challenges and related advice for fostering excellent mentoring relationships and psychologically healthy work environments:

• Adopt a humanistic approach to science.
            • Recognize that the culture of science is shifting toward collaboration.
            • Understand that experiences, perspectives, personalities, and worldviews vary. 
            • Realize that people are imperfect and make mistakes.
            • Take appropriate steps to make amends if you make a mistake.
            • Take time to calm down and respond pragmatically if someone upsets you. 
            • Adopt a mindset of continuous growth and improvement.

Adopt a Humanistic Approach to Science
It is tempting to think, “science is science,” meaning that science is objective, unbiased, and simply the process of following data to seek the truth. However, this mindset discounts the very real subjective, social, and cultural elements of science and the people who do it. Therefore, it is important to embrace rather than dismiss the “human side” of science. This humanistic approach is characterized by respect and compassion for everyone regardless of their career stage, role, or expertise (Branch Jr. et al. 2017; Gaufberg and Hodges 2016).

Recognize that the Culture of Science Is Shifting Toward Collaboration
The old stereotype of a lone individual in a lab is essentially defunct, as most research is team-based and collaborative. Funding agencies and organizations are taking the narrative of the abusive research supervisor who bullies and harasses mentees and other personnel seriously. Old stereotypes do not serve the research enterprise and do not represent most researchers who want to be effective mentors and create supportive environments.

Understand that Experiences, Perspectives, Personalities, and Worldviews Vary
The perspectives of people and tolerance levels for incivility differ. What people perceive as insensitive or uncivil differs from person to person. It is important to recognize that you may not know someone’s history, life experience, or expectations. People are human beings both inside and outside of work, and people bring their pasts with them to work. Leaders and their teams should strive to be respectful and supportive of these varying life experiences.

Realize that People Are Imperfect and Make Mistakes
While we must always strive to treat people well and with dignity and respect, we might make mistakes. Alternatively, we might be on the receiving end of uncivil or insensitive behavior. Sometimes, we need to understand that people make genuinely unintentional mistakes. In such cases, we can graciously hold them accountable through non-defensive communication so those mistakes do not happen again (Ellison 2016; Ford 2002). The focus should be more on patterns of overall behavior and less on isolated incidents where someone has a temporary lapse in judgment. Individuals may need the opportunity to learn from their mistakes and change their behavior.

Take Appropriate Steps to Make Amends If You Make a Mistake
Offer genuine apologies in the workplace when appropriate (Bisel and Messersmith 2012). Sincere, verbal acknowledgment that your words or behavior affected someone goes a long way toward repairing a relationship. Indicate the steps you plan to take to ensure you do not repeat the offense, and follow through on those plans. This self-accountability requires humility and can result in others having greater respect for you. Avoid excessively offering apologies in the workplace, however, as this diminishes an apology’s effect.

Take Time to Calm Down and Respond Pragmatically If Someone Upsets You
Taking time to calm down can help you think clearly and objectively (McIntosh et al. 2021). Try to see the problem from the other person’s perspective: Was it a genuine mistake? Were they stressed out? Then, communicate verbally with the person. Let them know specifically what happened, how it made you feel, and what you need from them moving forward. Give them an opportunity to explain and work together to mend the relationship. The focus should be on moving forward constructively, not placing blame. Ideally, you will receive an apology. However, people sometimes struggle to offer or accept apologies. It is worth noting that accepting an apology does not mean you accept the behavior. Rather, it indicates that you wish to move on in a constructive manner.

Adopt a Mindset of Continuous Growth and Improvement
The most important thing mentors and mentees can do is adopt a mindset of continuous growth and improvement. Building communication, interpersonal, conflict resolution, and leadership skills supports the skillful use of mentor and mentee practices and strategies. Developing these skills takes commitment, sustained effort, and practice and is worth the investment no matter one’s career stage or role (Antes and DuBois 2018). Reflection is a powerful tool for continuous professional development (Ashford and DeRue 2012; Porter 2017).

Three areas to continually focus on to be effective in mentoring relationships are as follows:

Build Your People Skills

Recognize that being an excellent mentor and mentee relies heavily on people skills like communication and perspective taking. Cultivating a positive research training environment also relies on leadership skills. Commit to improving these skills throughout your career. Take advantage of training opportunities that focus on mentoring and developing interpersonal and leadership skills like effective communication, conflict resolution, cultural awareness and sensitivity, and active listening.

Engage in Conflict Resolution

Recognize the challenges that arise in mentoring relationships and take ownership for handling them directly. Constructively addressing conflict can enhance and even repair relationships through mutual commitment and understanding. Do not let issues fester. Address them early and directly so they do not get worse. After taking time to calm down, ask to have a conversation with the person to convey your thoughts, understand their perspective, and work together to figure out how to resolve the conflict. Ensure that you listen actively and as much as or more than you speak.

Practice Reflection

Regularly self-assess what you are doing well and where you can improve as a mentor or mentee. This fosters continuous improvement in one’s practices and strategies. Set aside time on a regular basis to ask yourself what is working well and what can improve. For example, ask yourself, how frequently do you meet with your mentor or mentee? Are the meetings productive? How do you tend to deliver or receive critical feedback? How do you communicate and align expectations? Seek out advice from others when you identify areas for growth or change but are unsure where to begin.

Summary
Mentoring is critical to science. It affects the lives of mentors and mentees, the research training environment, the way research is conducted, and the future of the culture of science. Both mentors and mentees have key roles and responsibilities related to the mentoring relationship. While relationship-building, task-related, and situational challenges can emerge in mentoring relationships, mentors and mentees can adopt practices and strategies to cultivate healthy mentoring relationships and work through conflict constructively. Quality communication and mutual respect are essential for building and maintaining trust.

Mentors also play a critical role in shaping the work environment of the teams they lead. Mentees share responsibility for supporting a psychologically healthy environment. Mentors should intentionally establish a healthy and supportive work environment and lead by example.
