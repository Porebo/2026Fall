The four pivotal questions

Use these for every case, round-table bullet, and project proposal. Never stop at question 3.

# Question Role
1 Who is the customer? Who needs this information? (geology, compliance, reservoir eng, audit, field ops)
2 What was blocked? What decision, audit, report, or workflow could not proceed?
3 What did we deliver? What we did — keep brief (bot, load, rules, conversion, validation). AI/app lives here.
4 What did we enable? Headline for management. What became possible? This is value.
CUSTOMER → BLOCKED → DELIVERED → ENABLED who risk brief VALUE (headline)

Three intake questions (when requests lack context)

  1. What decision or workflow does this support? Who else uses it?
  2. Is there a deadline (audit, platform shutdown, planning cycle)?
  3. What breaks if this waits 30–60 days?

Project ≠ app ≠ value

Jarvis goal: show that AI usage creates value for the business — not that we shipped the most tools. Leadership is not running an app contest.

Making apps with AI has been interpreted as value generation. That is an illusion: apps are deliverables (question 3), not outcomes (question 4).

Tier What Maintenance
1 — Assist AI helps do the work; no new app None
2 — Tool Script/GUI for one workflow; named owner + sunset Bounded
3 — Product Rare; business + IT owns long-term Handoff required

Jarvis KPI (proposed): enabled workflows with named customer — not app count.

Rule: No app demos in leadership updates without a filled-in enabled line.

Five examples: the four questions in practice

Real patterns from normal operations (anonymized). Each case is filled in only with what we actually know. Gaps are shown on purpose — they teach what happens when intake or reporting stops early, and what you must ask before claiming value.

1. Wellbore path restoration (post-acquisition)

Janitorial trap: “AI bot fixed 87% of 14,700 wells in two weeks.”

1. Customer Regulatory compliance team (reporting focus on ~1,200 wells) and geologists performing reservoir characterization
2. Blocked After a recent acquisition, a large share of acquired wellbores lacked complete wellbore path data. Geologists could not find path information when needed. Compliance and characterization workflows were at risk of delay.
3. Delivered Automated correction bot restored path data on ~12,800 of 14,700 wells (~87%) in ~2 weeks. Manual estimate: two analysts × ~3 months. ~13% (~1,900 wells) still in progress.
4. Enabled Reservoir characterization and compliance reporting could continue without a multi-month integration delay while the remaining gap is closed.

Gaps (not yet stated): Dollar value of delay avoided; production volume (boe/d) tied to affected assets; named audit deliverable date; root cause of acquisition data gap.

2. Annual audit well counts

Janitorial trap: “Fixed 148 missing wells in an association table.”

1. Customer Internal audit team validating well counts for the annual audit
2. Blocked ~148 wells were not appearing in a well–completion association table, so the audit team’s reconciliation query could not produce a complete well inventory.
3. Delivered Investigation requested; remediation status not confirmed in the intake thread at time of documentation.
4. Enabled Likely: official well-inventory validation for annual audit can proceed. Cannot claim until delivered is confirmed.

Gaps (intake incomplete): Is remediation complete? Root cause? Audit submission deadline? This case arrived as email only — would not appear on a round-table slide unless someone completes the chain.

3. GIS / spatial integrity

Janitorial trap: “Updated field names and coordinates in the master system.”

1. Customer Operations / GIS users relying on spatial views for field assets (regional well portfolio)
2. Blocked Three wells never reached GIS; two wells plotted in the wrong state on spatial maps; field-name gaps prevented reliable lookup. Users could not trust map-based workflows.
3. Delivered Coordinate and field-name corrections in the master data system; partial reconciliation (thread closed with thanks — scope may be subset of full list).
4. Enabled GIS map integrity improved for the corrected wells so operational and planning views are not based on obviously wrong locations.

Gaps: Full well count reconciled (e.g. 10 vs. 12 reported); whether all three missing-from-GIS wells are resolved; quantified impact of wrong-state plotting; downstream systems affected beyond GIS.

4. BI platform migration

Janitorial trap: “Convert another dashboard to Spotfire.”

1. Customer Reservoir engineers (diatomite asset team) using a “constraint oil and gross” surveillance view
2. Blocked Legacy BI platform ends December 31, 2026. Without migration, the surveillance view is unavailable after cutoff.
3. Delivered Conversion requested and queued — not confirmed complete. Intake email named only the workbook.
4. Enabled Likely: diatomite constraint/gross surveillance continues after platform shutdown. Requires delivered + confirmation of who else uses the view and which planning cycle depends on it.

Gaps (classic thin request): No deadline stated in intake beyond enterprise cutoff; no list of other users; no description of decisions made from this view; no production or planning impact quantified. Three intake questions would fill most of this.

5. Call Out Sheet validation

Janitorial trap: “Built a 6-test validation GUI with AI-assisted PowerShell.”

1. Customer Staff who issue Call Out Sheets (field work instructions to service contractors); daily users identified in development
2. Blocked Mismatched well-header fields (API, name, field, location, datum, etc.) could reach contractors and downstream systems before anyone caught the error.
3. Delivered GUI validation app: reads Excel callout sheets, queries master well records, runs six checks, reports differences in seconds; built with enterprise-safe scripting (no external libraries).
4. Enabled Users can QC one or many callout sheets before release, reducing risk of bad field instructions entering downstream systems.

Gaps: Number of incidents or rework events prevented; cost of a wrong mobilization; volume of sheets processed per month; whether any contractor job was actually stopped by the tool. Without these, reporting should stop at “risk reduced before release” — not invent savings.

Teaching point: Example 1 is the most complete chain. Examples 2 and 4 show inbox work that never becomes a value story unless someone asks questions 1–4 before closing the ticket. Example 4 shows a request where only customer (partial) and blocked (via enterprise context) are known — delivered and enabled are still open.

Decisions from Jarvis leadership (staff choice)

Projects

~3 months ago each member proposed an AI project. After token limits stalled progress, staff may continue old projects or propose new ones. Justify with customer + enabled, not “I proposed it in May.”

Meeting cadence

Each team sets its own rhythm (weekly, biweekly, or hybrid). We decide together based on coordination need vs. focus time — not because another Jarvis team chose weekly.

Token drought

Former ~$20/month cap — some used it in 1–2 days and had no Claude access for the rest of the month. IT plan is now more generous. Rebaseline what is worth doing today.

Membership

Voluntary exit is allowed; license returns to the waiting list. No penalty. Not asking anyone to leave — talk to the team lead before stepping back.

Round-robin prompt (2 min each)

  1. Continue original project or propose new? One sentence why.
  2. Draft customer + enabled (even if rough).
  3. Cadence preference: weekly / biweekly / weekly async + biweekly live / 4-week trial.

Homework by follow-up date: half-page per person — continue or new project + four questions completed.

Full message to staff

Subject: Jarvis team — Zoom September 3: project choices, cadence, and how we measure success

Team,

Following the latest Jarvis leadership meeting, several decisions affect our team directly. We will align on Zoom September 3, 2026 before we set cadence and priorities together.

1. Projects: old ideas vs. new ones

Each of us was asked months ago to propose an AI project. Some Jarvis leaders want teams to keep pushing those original ideas. I argued — and the table agreed — that you should decide whether to:

  • Continue your original project if it still matters and is feasible, or
  • Propose something new if priorities, data, or access have changed.

For a period we had a $20/month Claude cap. Some people used that in a day or two and had no AI access for the rest of the month. IT has since improved the plan, but three-month-old project plans may not reflect today’s work.

Before or at the meeting: be ready to say continue or new, and draft customer + enabled using the four questions on this page.

2. Membership is voluntary

Jarvis agreed that anyone may step back voluntarily and return their Claude license to the waiting list. No penalty.

I am not asking anyone to leave. I want a committed team that sees value in this experiment. If participation does not fit your priorities, you may opt out — please talk to me first so we handle license and handoff cleanly.

3. A project is not the same as an app

Common belief: app = project = success. That is not what leadership is asking for.

Jarvis goal: show that AI usage creates value — better decisions, less delay, less risk — not that we shipped the most tools. An app may be one delivery method (Tier 2). Value is always question 4: what did we enable?

4. Meeting cadence — your input

Jarvis left cadence to each team. We will vote or agree on a trial (e.g. four weeks weekly, then revisit) based on how we actually work — not because other teams meet weekly.

We won staff choice on projects and cadence at the leadership table. Let’s use that to pursue work we can explain as blocked → enabled, not as app counts.

See you on Zoom September 3.

Closing line

“We won the right to choose our rhythm and our priorities. Let’s choose work we can explain in four sentences — customer, blocked, delivered, enabled — so Jarvis proves AI value the way leadership actually needs, not the way the app myth suggests.”
