Thesis Topic Exploration

the friction between formal data architectures and human/organizational behavior.

A thesis direction on the friction between enterprise data architecture, governance, and the informal organizational behaviors that determine whether those designs produce trusted information.

Draft Research Questions

  1. How do informal organizational behaviors affect the success of formal data architectures in upstream oil and gas environments?
  2. How do practitioners perceive the effect of executive leadership transitions on the sustainability of Information Quality governance?
  3. What types of workarounds emerge when enterprise data architecture does not align with local operational needs?
  4. How do incentives, deadlines, and ownership boundaries influence whether employees follow governed data processes?
  5. How can data architecture programs incorporate human behavior and organizational context into data quality design?
  6. What reporting practices help leadership see the value and risk implications of architecture-behavior misalignment?
  7. When an institutionalized Information Quality capability is dismantled, organizations may shift from preventive, enterprise-level IQ governance to reactive ticket-based remediation. This preserves local operations while allowing systemic data-quality debt to accumulate.
  8. How does the dismantling of a formal Information Quality capability reshape an organization’s data-quality work from preventive governance to reactive remediation?

Core idea: Data architectures are usually designed as formal systems of standards, pipelines, ownership, platforms, and controls. In practice, data quality is also shaped by incentives, local workarounds, tacit knowledge, deadline pressure, and how teams interpret responsibility.

This direction builds on the existing thesis notes about upstream data quality and value-based reporting. The technical problem is not only whether an architecture is correct on paper. The deeper research problem is why technically sound architectures still produce poor information outcomes when they collide with human and organizational behavior.

Related notes: Research Gaps in Data Quality for Upstream Oil Businesses and Value Framing Field Notes.

Possible Thesis Framing

Enterprise data architectures often assume that standardized models, governed platforms, common definitions, lineage, and formal stewardship roles will improve data quality. However, organizations are not controlled only by architecture. Employees route around slow systems, preserve spreadsheet-based knowledge, optimize for local deadlines, distrust centralized definitions, and report technical activity instead of decision value. These behaviors can weaken the intended benefits of formal data architecture.

A thesis could examine how formal architecture and informal behavior interact in upstream oil and gas data environments, especially during modernization, acquisitions, platform migrations, and analytics programs.

Why This Is Researchable

  • Architecture layer: canonical data models, master data, integration pipelines, governance councils, metadata, lineage, data catalogs, and quality rules.
  • Behavior layer: workarounds, shadow spreadsheets, undocumented tribal knowledge, tool distrust, incentive misalignment, role ambiguity, and request intake habits.
  • Outcome layer: data defects, delayed decisions, duplicate work, low adoption, audit risk, misreported value, and reduced confidence in analytics.

The topic is strong because it connects information quality theory with observable workplace behavior. It can be studied through interviews, document analysis, anonymized field notes, process mapping, or a case-study design.

Research Gap

Much data architecture literature focuses on frameworks, platforms, governance structures, and technical integration patterns. Much organizational behavior literature explains incentives, culture, change management, and informal practice. The gap is the intersection: how informal organizational behavior changes the actual data quality outcomes of formal data architecture.

In upstream oil and gas, this gap matters because architecture decisions affect reservoir characterization, production surveillance, regulatory reporting, emissions tracking, and AI-enabled operations. The cost of mismatch is not only messy data; it is slower or less reliable operational decision-making.

Methods That Fit

Case study

Study one organization, program, migration, acquisition, or data domain. This is likely the strongest fit if using anonymized professional observations.

Interview-based qualitative study

Interview data stewards, analysts, engineers, architects, and business users about how they experience architecture, governance, and workarounds.

Document and artifact analysis

Compare formal artifacts such as data models, standards, intake forms, governance decks, and quality rules against informal artifacts such as email requests, spreadsheet trackers, exception logs, and team slide decks.

Sharpened Version

A practical thesis could ask: How do informal workarounds and incentive structures affect the data quality outcomes of formal data architecture programs in upstream oil and gas organizations?

This wording keeps the topic narrow enough for a master’s thesis while preserving the richer idea: architecture is not only a technical design problem. It is also a behavioral system.
