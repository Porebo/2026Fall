Student Question

Why Does “Do” (Quality Control) Precede “Check” (Quality Assurance)?

An online student emailed a sharp observation: in Plan-Do-Check-Act, the Do step is quality control (checking the finished product), while Check is quality assurance (checking the inputs/parts going in) — which seems backwards, since in a build process you'd assure the inputs before you ever get to a finished product to control.

The resolution ties directly back to the course's central principle: manage information as product. In the planning and design phase, you must start by defining the product — its requirements are quality control — before you can know what quality assurance checks the inputs actually need. You can't design input checks for data you don't yet know the product requires. Once planning is complete and you're actually running the process, assurance naturally happens before control in time — but in the design sequence, control has to come first.

Working the Current Assignment

Fitness for Use, Not Just “Clean vs. Dirty”

Reviewing the grade-report requirements exercise: some anomalies in a file are intrinsic and obvious regardless of use (missing data, inconsistent representation). But most judgments about quality require knowing how the data will be used. A column with heavy missing values isn't automatically low quality — if that column was never going to be used in the product being built, its completeness was never a requirement in the first place. Quality, per the ISO definition, is meeting requirements — and requirements can only be written once you know what you're building.

The professor also pushed back on equating data quality purely with “garbage in, garbage out”: sometimes good, already-clean data creates new value simply by being combined with other data in a new way, without any cleansing at all. Example: the Night Sky astronomy app doesn't generate any new astronomical data — positions of celestial bodies have been plotted for centuries. What's new is combining a phone's geolocation and orientation sensors with that existing table to show, instantly, exactly what should be in the sky wherever the phone is pointed. That's an information product built entirely from recombination, not data cleanup.

Student Demo

Comparing Two Database Replacement Candidates

A student shared a live work example directly applying Chapter 4 concepts: a production database (“AWS”) is being retired, with two candidate replacements to evaluate for whether they contain equivalent data.

  • Nine category groups of columns were each scored on completeness (e.g. identity fields: 100% in the source, 99.9% in both candidates) and accuracy, defined here as the percentage of populated rows where a candidate's value matches the original AWS value — one candidate matched 98%, the other 100% on a given category.
  • A weighted formula combined both scores per category to recommend which candidate to use, with some categories favoring one candidate and some the other — pointing toward a likely hybrid recommendation rather than picking a single winner outright.
  • The professor clarified an important nuance: this “accuracy” measure isn't proving either candidate is accurate in any absolute sense — it's measuring agreement with a trusted benchmark (the retiring AWS system), which is itself a legitimate and common way to operationalize accuracy when ground truth isn't otherwise available.
  • An open question raised but not yet resolved: instead of choosing one candidate category-by-category, could the two candidates be combined (e.g. via a SQL union) to produce a single score better than either alone?
