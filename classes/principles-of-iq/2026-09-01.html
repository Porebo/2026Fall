Core Concept

Two Ways to Relate Information to Value

Every organization uses data, and every organization has problems. IQ work lives in the intersection — not every problem is a data problem (some are cultural or communication issues), and not every data defect is a business problem.

  1. Start with the business issue. Business issue → is it related to data? → find the root cause in the data → improve data quality → improve business results. Example: invoices not reaching customers because addresses are wrong, out of date, or incomplete.
  2. Start with the data quality assessment. Assess data against requirements → find anomalies → determine whether the anomaly actually impacts the business → if so, improve data quality → improve business results.

Critical caveat: a data quality anomaly does not automatically mean a business problem. Any relational database has fully null tables nobody uses. Sweeping assessments are time-consuming and labor-intensive, and “perfect data everywhere” is not a goal — starting from business impact gives you a specific target to fix.

Framework

Where to Find Information Value

  • Financial: operating costs, revenue, cash flow, charges, budget.
  • Compliance: regulatory reporting (education, medical, financial), government and industry requirements, privacy.
  • Confidence & trust: if people don’t trust the data they won’t use it, and unused data produces no value.
  • Use: decisions, system control, forecasting, reporting.
  • Satisfaction: customer experience, suppliers, employees.
  • Risk: regulatory penalties, credit, investment, competitiveness, data leakage (“two kinds of companies: those that have been breached and those that don’t know it yet”).
  • Productivity: LLMs and agentic AI improving workflows — agents as “little employees” inside the model, all requiring context, which is data.
  • Health: disease control, treatments, mental health, diet, fitness.
  • Safety: environmental protection, accident prevention, self-driving cars, traffic flow, vehicle safety.

Reminder: data has potential value at rest, but it only produces value when it is used.

Quantitative Methods

Value, ROI, and Net Present Value

Value = benefits − cost. Cost can be money, time, wear and tear — anything you must give up to act.

Return on investment is the net gain as a percentage of the amount invested:

ROI = ((benefits − cost) / cost) × 100%

ROI applies to financial, medical, and mission endeavors alike, and it is the standard way to compare competing improvement opportunities when you cannot fund them all.

Discounting: the time value of money

For anything longer than a year, use discounted values for cost and revenue. The assumption is that money should always be working — uninvested money loses value. The annual discount rate varies by industry.

  • At a 5% annual return, $100 paid one year from now is worth $100 / 1.05 ≈ $95 today.
  • Future dollars are worth less today — this is the net present value (NPV).

Lottery example

A “$1,000,000 jackpot” paid as $100,000 today plus $100,000 per year for 9 more years is not worth $1,000,000. At a 10% discount rate it is worth about $675,000 today. Annuity-buying companies compute exactly this, then offer you the discounted value minus their profit.

Excel formulas

  • NPV(rate, value1, value2, ...) — use when payments differ; list each future payment. Add the immediate payment separately.
  • PV(rate, nper, pmt, 0) — use when all payments are equal; no need to list each one. The trailing 0 excludes the immediate first payment.

Worked Example

Timing Changes ROI: SPOT Reference Data Project

A not-for-profit has inconsistent reference tables across operating units, causing costly erroneous shipments. The proposed fix is a single point of truth (SPOT) corporate reference — master data. Implementation takes 4 years: total cost $120,000, total benefit $160,000.

Simple ROI, ignoring time: ($160,000 − $120,000) / $120,000 = 33% in every scenario. But timing changes everything (5% annual discount rate). Costs and benefits by year, in thousands of dollars:

  • Scenario 1 — costs 45, 35, 25, 15; benefits 10, 20, 40, 90. NPV ROI = 26%
  • Scenario 2 — costs 45, 35, 25, 15; benefits 90, 40, 20, 10. NPV ROI = 36%
  • Scenario 3 — costs 15, 25, 35, 45; benefits 90, 40, 20, 10. NPV ROI = 40%

Interpretation: Scenario 1 is worst — high costs now, benefits far out (and therefore discounted). Scenario 3 is best — benefits now, costs pushed into cheaper future dollars. Scenario 2 tracks close to the naive 33% because costs and benefits move together.

Professional Practice

Making the Business Case for Information Quality

The prevailing view treats IQ as janitorial work — cleaning up dirty data, a pure expense. The business case reframes it as an investment that returns more than it costs.

  • Argues that the IQ improvement will succeed and create value.
  • Shifts perception from added expense to investment.
  • Gains management support, which unlocks the resources a project needs to succeed.
  • Must align explicitly with organizational goals and objectives — for businesses, healthcare, government, and nonprofits alike.

Consider all the factors

  • Time required, and whether the benefit is short-term or long-term.
  • Risk of failure — could the project return nothing?
  • Resource availability: right people in the right roles, IT infrastructure, data experts, project leaders.
  • Visibility — will people see and understand the results? IQ practitioners rarely blow their own horn.

Framework

The Information Quality Context

Any data issue has four components, not one. Data alone is never the whole picture:

  1. Data — what is in it, and which items matter most. Critical data elements (CDEs) are identified by asking what would happen to the process or organization if that element disappeared, and what recovery would cost.
  2. People — those who use, manipulate, and manage the data. Many IQ issues are people issues, which is why the program includes a Project and Change Management course.
  3. Process — how data is transformed into a product or service.
  4. Technology — the specific tools (IBM, Informatica, SAP) that execute those processes, with different efficiencies for different work.

Decision Tool

Cost-Benefit Matrix

Plot each opportunity on a two-dimensional matrix: benefit on the y-axis, cost on the x-axis. Ranking of the quadrants:

  1. High benefit / low cost — first choice.
  2. High benefit / high cost — second choice; always chase the most benefit available.
  3. Low benefit / low cost — third choice; easy but small.
  4. Low benefit / high cost — last choice, though still net positive.

Benefits can mean increased revenue, lower operational cost, or avoided fines and penalties. Costs can mean additional software or personnel.

Worked Example

Qualitative vs. Quantitative Justification

Qualitative (weak): “Project A will improve information product X, which will increase customer satisfaction.” No numbers, so management just sees a spending request.

Quantitative (strong): the same project, expressed as measurable dollars:

  • Expected improvement: customer retention up 10% per year.
  • Cost to acquire a new customer (advertising, onboarding): $1,200.
  • Cost to service an existing customer per year: $400.
  • Current customer base: 2,000.

2,000 × 10% × ($1,200 − $400) = $160,000 per year

Any project cost below $160,000 is a winning formula. In business this write-up is called a pro forma: show the money coming back over time, even if the inputs are estimates.

Framework

POSMAD: The Information Life Cycle

All data has a life cycle, but lengths vary wildly: stock quotes last seconds; a date of birth stays valid for as long as the person is a customer or patient. Danette McGilvra’s Executing Data Quality Projects gives the mnemonic POSMAD:

  1. Plan — do we even need this information? What will it cost? Compile it ourselves, buy it, or lease it? Check use restrictions on licensed data (marketing-only data cannot become permanent reference data). Request vendor samples (e.g., specific ZIP codes) to compare quality and quantity across vendors.
  2. Obtain — create/compile or acquire. Compiling example: 2,000 U.S. telephone directories were unbound, shipped overseas, and double-keyed by two independent operators with a third resolving discrepancies — near-100% keying accuracy. Loading is expensive: 825 employees at Acxiom did nothing but inspect incoming files, identify fields, and map them to the standard format.
  3. Store & share — design storage and access. A double-edged sword: unused data creates no value, but over-sharing creates leakage risk.
  4. Maintain — keep it current (people move, phones and emails change). Common operations: parsing (splitting a name into first/middle/last/suffix), standardization (postal abbreviations like ST for Street), enhancement (e.g., gender inference from names), deduplication and merging (Bill Smith vs. William Smith; merging patient encounters into a single medical chart).
  5. Apply — build the information product or service that creates value.
  6. Dispose — archive or destroy. Some regulations require retention for 7 years and destruction afterward.

The key insight: all six phases cost money, but only Apply produces benefit. The apply phase must carry all the others — which is exactly why the business case must show positive ROI.

Not linear: Plan and Obtain come first, but Maintain and Apply run in a continuous cycle — monthly data feeds arriving while a website serves users in real time.

Emerging Topics

Unstructured Data & LLM Extraction

Telephone directories are semi-structured; corporate action filings are unstructured narrative forms describing ownership changes with no fields to parse. Extracting “who bought whom, and when” historically required a human reader.

  • LLMs can now read documents and extract structured information — one of the major current applications of AI in data quality.
  • Extends to audio (speech-to-text captions, as in this recording) and images (OCR, object detection).

Core Concept

Information Is a Unique Resource

The manufacturing analogy is useful — raw data in, processing machinery, finished information product — but it breaks down in two places:

  1. Tolerances are not intuitive. In manufacturing, diameter, tensile strength, and weight are physical and understandable. Data quality dimensions like timeliness are far less obvious.
  2. Raw material is not consumed. Steel is used up building cars; data can be copied and reused infinitely.
    • Upside: marginal value increases with use — acquire once, sell copies many times.
    • Downside: copies of bad data are bad data, replicated everywhere. Spilled oil can be cleaned up; spilled data can never be recalled.

Data is non-fungible. One barrel of a given crude is interchangeable with another; barrels have no identity. Data describes entities — customers, patients, products — that are unique and separately identifiable, so records are not interchangeable.
