Session Focus

From Manufacturing Quality to Information Quality

Information quality adapted ideas from the manufacturing quality movement to the creation of information products and services. The major transition came through data warehousing, MIT research, and consulting work that treated information as something produced, managed, and delivered to customers.

  • Manufacturing quality supplied the methods for controlling processes and improving products.
  • Data warehousing exposed large-scale information quality problems in organizations.
  • Information quality applies quality management to data, people, processes, technology, and the value delivered to users.
  • Agentic data quality is an emerging phase that uses AI agents to help assess and improve data.

Manufacturing Foundations

Shewhart and Statistical Process Control

Walter Shewhart pioneered statistical process control: repeatedly sampling production over time and looking for variation outside expected control limits.

  • Data has no meaning apart from its context.
  • Processes contain both signal and noise, so anomalies must be distinguished from normal variation.
  • Quality control checks the finished product against requirements.
  • Quality assurance acts during the process to increase the likelihood that the finished product will meet those requirements.
  • The Plan-Do-Check-Act cycle is associated with Shewhart; Deming later adapted it as Plan-Do-Study-Act.

Continuous recording and analysis of failures support continual process improvement rather than one-time inspection.

Quality Management

Deming and Continuous Improvement

W. Edwards Deming promoted process thinking, statistical process control, and organizational transformation. He argued that management and the design of the process are responsible for most quality problems, rather than individual workers alone.

  • His system of profound knowledge connected variation, systems, knowledge, and psychology.
  • His 14 points addressed organizational transformation.
  • He warned against short-term profit pressure, fear-based performance appraisals, management mobility, visible quotas, and unstable purpose.
  • Kaizen, the Japanese idea of change for the better, expresses the goal of continuous improvement.

Quality Trilogy

Juran: Planning, Improvement, and Control

Joseph Juran’s quality trilogy consists of quality planning, quality improvement, and quality control. His idea of fitness for use applies primarily to the finished product or service and the requirements of its users.

  • Plan around the customer: who are the customers, what do they need, and how will the product be delivered?
  • Improve processes through study, error reduction, root cause analysis, and breakthrough thinking.
  • Control the final product by comparing actual performance with planned performance.
  • Use statistical sampling when inspecting every product is impractical.

For information products, data quality requirements concern input characteristics such as accuracy and completeness, while information quality requirements concern the value and usefulness of the finished product.

Modern Quality Practice

TQM, Prevention, and Root Causes

Feigenbaum and total quality management

Armand Feigenbaum popularized Total Quality Control and Total Quality Management. The word total means everyone involved in the process considers how their work contributes to the customer’s finished product. He also described the hidden plant: the extra work and workarounds needed to repair defects in an ordinary process.

Crosby and doing it right the first time

Philip Crosby’s claim that “quality is free” means that the investment in prevention can be recovered through a more valuable product and lower costs of nonconformance. His DIRFT principle means “doing it right the first time.” Prevention usually costs less than repairing, recalling, or replacing a defective product later.

Ishikawa and the fishbone diagram

Kaoru Ishikawa emphasized user-friendly quality control, quality circles, internal customers, and root cause analysis. His fishbone diagram helps teams explore possible causes of a problem across people, methods, materials, machines, measurement, and environment.

Japanese Quality Movement

Shingo and Taguchi

  • Shigeo Shingo: contributed to the Toyota Production System, just-in-time production, lean production, and reducing unnecessary inventory, effort, and time.
  • Genichi Taguchi: developed the Taguchi loss function, which measures the broader financial and social loss caused by poor quality, not just the immediate cost of correcting one defect.
  • Taguchi also promoted design of experiments and designs less sensitive to parameters outside an engineer’s control.

The broader lesson is that a small data error can produce a much larger downstream loss when it affects budgets, decisions, contracts, or other information products.

Information Quality Practice

From Data Quality to Information Products

Larry English brought quality ideas into data warehousing and developed the POSMAD information life cycle: Plan, Obtain, Store and share, Maintain, Apply, and Dispose.

Richard Wang adapted total quality management to total data quality management and established the Wang & Strong framework of 16 data quality dimensions. The central idea is to manage information as a product: identify the product and its users first, then work backward to determine the data and process requirements.

Tom Redman extended this thinking through the cost of poor data quality, information flow, and the Rule of 10: defective input data can make a unit of work cost an order of magnitude more than it would with correct input data.

Danette McGilvra’s project-oriented framework and Laura Sebastian-Coleman’s measurement practices provide additional ways to structure and sustain data quality improvement.

Application to This Course

Start with the Product and Its Users

Cleaning data is not enough to guarantee a quality information product. The product, its users, and their requirements determine what data is needed and what condition that data must be in.

  • Good input data does not guarantee a good output if the process or product requirements are wrong.
  • Garbage in, garbage out is one-way: bad inputs can produce bad outputs, but good inputs do not automatically produce valuable outputs.
  • Quality improvement should prevent errors early, measure the cost of nonconformance, and investigate root causes.
  • Zero defects can be a useful aspiration, but the practical question is whether the product meets the requirements of its users and creates value.

  5. Explain the difference between data quality requirements and information product value requirements.
