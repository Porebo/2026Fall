Inspection and Sampling

Quality-control ideas inherited from manufacturing also apply to information products. A small number of reports can be inspected individually; when output volume rises, sampling may be more practical. The lecture compares systematic, stratified, and cluster sampling, including an example of inspecting every twentieth box. (~00:00–15:30)

Quality issues can also cross organizational boundaries: the discussion cites a space-mission example involving incompatible units exchanged between teams. The amount of inspection should be considered alongside its cost and the consequences of defects. (~20:00)

Finding and Prioritizing Causes

When defects appear in outputs, trace them back to input data or processes; experiments can test how sensitive outputs are to changes in input quality. A Pareto chart orders categories by frequency and can show cumulative frequency to help prioritize investigation. The 80/20 pattern is a heuristic, not a guarantee. (~25:00–30:45)

Control Charts

Control charts help distinguish common-cause variation from signals that may indicate an assignable or special cause. A signal can be a false alarm, and a chart can also fail to detect a real special cause. Interpret the chart in the context of the process rather than treating every fluctuation as a defect. (~35:00–46:30)

Variable charts track measured values or statistics such as sample means and ranges; attribute charts track counts or proportions. An np chart uses counts of defective items, while a p chart uses proportions and can accommodate varying sample sizes. (~46:45–50:55)
