Population, Sample, and Sampling Error

A population is the full group of interest; a sample is a subset examined when measuring every member is impractical. A sample statistic, such as a sample mean, estimates a population parameter. The difference between the estimate and the true parameter is sampling error. A sample is useful only to the extent that its selection is appropriate for the question. (~05:00–10:45, 45:00)

  • Simple random sampling: identify population members and select using random numbers. (~15:00)
  • Systematic sampling: select every nth record. Ordering or periodic patterns can cause important problems to be missed. (~20:00)
  • Stratified sampling: divide the population into meaningful groups and sample within them. The degree-plan example separates cases with and without transfer credits and major changes, including rare but potentially problematic combinations. (~25:00)
  • Cluster sampling: select groups that represent larger segments of the population, as in polling examples. (~30:30)

Frequency and Center

Frequency tables count values or ranges of values. Histograms and frequency polygons make the shape of a distribution easier to see than a list of observations. (~35:00–40:30)

The mean uses all observations; the median is the middle of the ordered values; the mode is the most frequent value. A dataset can have more than one mode or no repeated value. Compare mean and median when extreme observations could distort the mean. (~45:00–50:50, ~60:00)

Spread and Standard Deviation

The range uses only the smallest and largest observations, so it can conceal how the rest are distributed. Standard deviation summarizes dispersion using squared deviations from the mean and then a square root. The population calculation divides by N; the usual sample calculation divides by n − 1. Identify whether the observations are the entire population or a sample before choosing the calculation. (~60:00–70:40)
