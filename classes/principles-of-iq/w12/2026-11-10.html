Assessment in Context

The class revisits data-quality requirements and measurement before discussing AI-assisted techniques. Data-quality assessment examines inputs; information quality asks whether the resulting product or service meets consumer requirements and creates value. (~00:00–05:45)

Profiling and Missing Values

Column profiles can expose unexpected values, such as a code outside an expected set. A non-null value is not necessarily a meaningful value: placeholders such as question marks and “no middle name” may conceal missing information. Numeric zero can also be ambiguous when a source uses it to represent missing data. (~10:00–20:45)

Pattern profiling abstracts characters into classes, such as uppercase and lowercase letters, to reveal inconsistent formats. Sorting must respect data types: text sorting can place “1000” before “99”, unlike numeric sorting; dates stored as strings can likewise mislead. (~25:00–30:45)

Relationships and Standardization

Primary and foreign keys connect rows across tables. For example, a product ID in a sales record should refer to a corresponding product. Such relationships allow checks that cannot be made from one column alone. (~35:00)

Standardization aims for consistent representations, but must preserve meaning. The lecture discusses date and address formats, aliases such as Bob and Robert, and accent marks that can change a word's meaning. AI can help structure documents or interpret variants, but its results still need quality assessment. (~45:00–60:40)
