Applied Concepts — INFQ 70303

From Janitorial Metrics to Value Framing

Study guide connecting John Talburt’s Principles of IQ lectures (Aug 25 & 27, 2026) to field practice. Anonymized examples from upstream oil & gas data work.

The Source

Talburt’s Value Chain (Aug 27 Lecture)

Everything on this page descends from one segment of the August 27 lecture. Read the original before the adaptations below.

The definition: “We create value whenever the benefits outweigh or [are] larger than the investment that it takes to get that benefit.” Value must be positive, and it is measured in exactly three units: monetary, quality of life, or mission accomplishment.

The failure mode: “We often confuse results with value... we expend an effort, we make results, we get some kind of big improvement that we measure, but what is the value of that?”

Talburt’s Acxiom story: his team raised integration precision from 88% to 92% and recall from 70% to 80% — a hard-won lift. The team rejoiced; the executives’ eyes rolled. “We assumed they understood... but we never said, how. How is it gonna be better for the business?”

The test for telling them apart

“Results, we talk about as statistics, as percentages. But value is... increasing revenue, or lowering cost, or lowering mortality... higher success rates, and mission accomplishment.”

If it carries a % sign, it is a result. If it is denominated in money, lives, or mission, it is value.

His worked example — note the three chained results before value appears

  1. Effort: 5 man-year system development, $1M direct cost + $500K indirect cost.
  2. Result: new customer recognition system, 15% more accurate than legacy.
  3. Result: survey shows customer satisfaction up 20%.
  4. Result: analysis shows customer retention up 8%.
  5. Value: the gap between customer acquisition cost and customer maintenance cost projects $20M net revenue over 5 years — more than covering the $1.5M spent.

His instruction: “We need to keep going with the value chain until we get from results to a value.” He does not jump. Three results sit between the technical win and the dollar figure.

The direction of reasoning is backwards

From Principle 1: “It’s understanding what customers need... what do they need and want and that they will pay for? How do we make it a quality product or service? And then we can work backwards and see what data, what condition does the data need to be in.”

The anti-pattern he names: “We don’t want to think about information quality as just cleaning up dirty data. Nobody’s excited about that... we gotta have janitors.”

Core Lesson

What Talburt Wants You to Remember

Information quality is about creating value and minimizing risk — not celebrating data cleaning as the product.

  • Data quality (inward): Is the data right? Measured in %, completeness, conformance.
  • Information quality (outward): Are we building the right thing? Measured in decisions enabled, risk reduced, mission, quality of life.
  • Don’t confuse results with value. “Fixed 87% of errors” is a result. “Geologists resumed reservoir characterization” is an intermediate outcome. “Avoided ~6 analyst-months of manual rework” is value.
  • Historically, the field started with janitorial cleansing (1980s warehousing). Mature IQ moves to product thinking, governance, and enterprise value.

Framework

The Value Chain, Adapted for Field Reporting

Talburt’s chain runs effort → results → value. This is that chain restated for data-team reporting, with his front-end question (who is the customer, and what do they need?) made explicit:

  1. Customer — Who needs the information? (Principle 1: start here and work backwards.)
  2. Blocked — What decision or process was at risk? (Risk minimization, the second half of the definition.)
  3. Delivered — What we did. Keep brief. This is effort.
  4. Enabled — What became possible? This is an intermediate result, not the destination.
  5. Valued — How much, in money, quality of life, or mission? This is the headline.

Rule: Never stop at step 3, and do not mistake step 4 for the end. Metrics like “% fixed” or “wells loaded” are footnotes.

The discipline test on step 5: if you cannot state it in dollars, lives, or mission accomplishment, you are still reporting results. Estimates and guesstimates are acceptable — Talburt calls this a pro forma — but the number must be there.

Delivery Modes

Apps When Needed — Value Always

Apps, scripts, and GUIs are legitimate and often the right answer when a workflow repeats, multiple users need guardrails, or bad data must be caught before release (see Call Out Sheet validation below).

Not every AI win requires an app. Assist-only work — analysis, drafting, one-time investigation — can deliver the same value faster when repeatability is not the issue.

The reporting mistake is not building apps; it is stopping at “we built a tool” without stating what was enabled, or forcing every project into app form when assist would suffice.

Anonymized Examples

Result, Outcome, Value

Each example is graded against Talburt’s test. The third line is the one most reports never write.

Wellbore path restoration (post-acquisition)

  • Result (janitorial): “Bot fixed 87% of errors on 14,700 wells in two weeks.” Carries a %, so it is a result.
  • Outcome: Compliance and geology teams regained wellbore path data for regulatory reporting and reservoir characterization; ~12,800 wells restored.
  • Value: Manual remediation would have taken two analysts ~3 months — roughly 6 analyst-months of loaded labor avoided, plus regulatory exposure from late reporting. Monetary proposition.

Annual audit well counts

  • Result (janitorial): “Investigated 148 missing wells in an association table.”
  • Outcome: Internal audit can now reconcile well counts for the annual audit.
  • Value: Avoided an audit finding on well inventory. Quantify as the cost of a remediation cycle plus penalty exposure — risk minimization, the second half of Talburt’s definition.

GIS / spatial integrity

  • Result (janitorial): “Fixed field names and coordinates in the master system.”
  • Outcome: Wells missing from GIS restored; two wells plotted in the wrong state corrected.
  • Value: Location integrity protects every downstream decision that reads the map — permitting, spacing, mobilization. Quantify as the cost of one misrouted field decision.

BI platform migration (Tableau exit)

  • Result (janitorial): “Converted another dashboard to Spotfire.”
  • Outcome: Reservoir engineers retain constraint and surveillance views past the December 31, 2026 platform end-of-life.
  • Value: Business continuity for surveillance decisions. Quantify as the production impact of engineers losing constraint visibility, not as dashboards converted.

Call Out Sheet validation app

  • Result (janitorial): “Built a GUI with six validation tests in PowerShell.”
  • Outcome: Bad well-header instructions are caught before they reach field contractors.
  • Value: Cost of one wrong mobilization avoided per catch — crew, equipment, and rework. Technology is the footnote; wrong mobilization is the risk.

Pattern: in every case the janitorial line is a percentage or a count, the outcome line describes capability, and only the third line carries a unit Talburt would accept.

Practice

When Requests Lack Context

Customers often ask for tools (“convert this dashboard”) without stating impact. This is Talburt’s backward reasoning applied at intake — start from what the customer needs, then work backwards to the data condition required:

  1. What decision or workflow does this support? Who else uses it?
  2. Is there a deadline (audit, platform shutdown, planning cycle)?
  3. What breaks if this waits 30–60 days?
  4. What does that breakage cost — in money, safety, or mission? (Forces step 5 of the chain.)

Talburt’s warning on skipping this: without a feedback loop from the user, teams build “gold-plated” products nobody asked for. He calls the missing loop the information quality gap, and the fix is an information product manager acting as liaison between consumer and producer.
