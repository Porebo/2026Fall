Binomial and Normal Examples

For five independent fair coin flips, exactly four heads has probability 5/32, approximately 15.6%. The same binomial framework can describe counts of survey responses when the trial assumptions hold. (~05:00–10:30)

For a normal variable with mean 10 and standard deviation 2, the threshold 13 corresponds to a z-score of 1.5; its cumulative probability is about 93%. Interval probabilities come from subtracting cumulative probabilities. About 68.27% of a normal distribution lies within one standard deviation of its mean. (~15:00–30:45)

Correlation and Regression

The lecture uses seniority and salary to illustrate a linear relationship and predictions from a fitted line. The example line is approximately salary = 1.97 × seniority + 34.95; substituting eight years gives about 50.7 in the example's salary units. Least-squares fitting minimizes the sum of squared vertical residuals. (~40:00–49:00)

Clarification: the discussion identifies the Pearson correlation coefficient with the slope. In general they are not the same: for simple linear regression the slope is r × (sy/sx). Correlation describes standardized association and is between −1 and 1.

Sampling Distribution

The central limit theorem motivates inference using the distribution of sample means. For independent observations under suitable conditions, that distribution approaches normality as sample size grows, with mean equal to the population mean and standard error σ/√n. The lecture uses a consumer-spending example to compare an observed sample mean with a proposed population mean. (~55:00–65:45)
