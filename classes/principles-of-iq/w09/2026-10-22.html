Conditioning and Rare Events

The condition after “given” defines the population used as the denominator. The lecture revisits a rare-gene test: a prior of one in 1,000, a 3% false-positive rate, and a 2% false-negative rate. Even a positive test does not imply a 98% chance of the gene; the prior and both test rates must be combined. (~05:00–15:45)

In the patient-record example, six records yield 15 unordered pairs. Some pairs refer to the same person even with different names, and identical names can belong to different people. P(same patient | same name) and P(same name | same patient) use different denominators. (~20:00–35:45)

Discrete and Binomial Distributions

A random variable assigns numerical values to outcomes. For two dice, the sum is a random variable: different sums have different counts among the 36 possible ordered outcomes. The probabilities are symmetric around 7. (~40:00–45:45)

The binomial model counts successes across a fixed number of independent trials with the same success probability. The coin example asks about the number of heads in five flips. In Excel, the cumulative option distinguishes the probability of exactly a specified number of successes from the probability up to that number. (~50:00–55:45)

Continuous Distributions

For a continuous variable, probabilities are assigned to intervals using the area under a density curve, rather than to individual exact values. A normal distribution is determined by its mean and standard deviation. The cumulative normal function provides the probability up to a threshold; subtract two cumulative values to obtain the probability between thresholds. (~60:00–75:45)
