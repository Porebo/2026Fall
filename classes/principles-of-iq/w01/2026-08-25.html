Core Concept

What Is Information Quality?

Information quality is an information science discipline rooted in applying Total Quality Management (TQM) principles to building information products.

  • IQ evolved from 1980s manufacturing TQM (Deming in Japan; Toyota quality) applied to data management at MIT and in industry.
  • Common definitions include accuracy, fitness for use, completeness, and “right information, right time, right place.”
  • The program’s working definition: manufacturing information products from raw data using computers, managed like physical products in manufacturing.
  • Two primary goals: maximize value and minimize risk of an organization’s information assets.
  • Value applies both to the organization producing information and to the consumers of that information (e.g., transcripts, medical charts, state reporting).

Distinction

Information Quality vs. Data Quality

Information quality is broader than data quality.

  • Data quality: data meeting defined requirements (ISO definition of quality = meeting requirements). Example: measuring missing or inaccurate zip codes in a customer database.
  • Information quality: also includes value creation, strategy, governance, environment, and culture — not just cleaning dirty data.
  • Poor information/data quality costs organizations billions annually; outdated or wrong information can have serious real-world consequences (e.g., a military targeting error that bombed a hospital instead of headquarters because geographic data was not updated).
  • AI models depend on training data quality; agentic data quality uses AI agents to improve data quality (covered in labs this semester).

Framework

Six Domains of Information Quality Knowledge & Practice

Developed by IQ International (IQCP — Information Quality Certified Professional):

  1. Value & organizational impact — not limited to business; includes nonprofits, hospitals, and government.
  2. Strategy & governance — data strategy, chief data officer role, regulatory compliance (e.g., FERPA at UA Little Rock).
  3. Environment & culture — organizational change; examples like Kodak failing to adapt to digital photography.
  4. Measurement & improvement — you cannot manage what you cannot measure; sustained programs, not one-time projects.
  5. Information architecture — quality depends on architecture (client-server, big data, agentic AI architectures).
  6. Data quality — requirements, metrics, completeness, accuracy, and improvement projects.

The first three domains focus on management of information, not dirty-data metrics alone. Related standards discussed in the course include ISO 9000 (TQM), ISO 8000 (data quality), and the emerging ISO 25500 family.
