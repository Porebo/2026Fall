Core Concept

What Is Information Quality?

Common street-level answers focus on accuracy, but IQ is broader. Other frequently cited attributes include completeness, timeliness, and freedom from defects.

  • Fitness for use / fitness for purpose — from Joseph Juran (TQM). Originally referred to the finished product, not raw source data.
  • Program definition (two parts): maximize value and minimize risk of an organization’s information assets.
  • Internal view: how data creates value for the organization that holds it.
  • External view: whether information products and services create value for consumers (people or systems).
  • Danette McGilvra emphasizes trusted data in her 10-step method; value and trust are closely related perspectives.

Framework

Three Value Propositions

Value = benefits outweighing investment. Value is not only monetary:

  1. Monetary — profit, revenue, cost reduction (typical business view).
  2. Quality of life — hospitals, physicians, medicines; improving human well-being.
  3. Mission accomplishment — nonprofits, government, military; achieving organizational missions regardless of dollar metrics.

Don’t confuse results with value. Technical results (precision 88% → 92%, recall 70% → 80%) are not the same as business value. Complete the value chain: results → intermediate outcomes → measurable value (e.g., customer retention → projected $20M revenue over 5 years).

History

Evolution of Information Quality

IQ emerged primarily from industry consultants, not academia. Jan Lee (Journey to Data Quality) traces roots even further (e.g., the Rosetta Stone), but the modern field begins with data warehousing in the 1980s.

  1. Data cleansing phase — 1980s data warehousing exposed inconsistent representation (e.g., “AR” vs. “ARK” for Arkansas). Larry English and Information Impact International led early warehouse cleanup work.
  2. Prevention phase — root cause analysis: fix inconsistent sources, not just warehouse symptoms. Parallels Deming, Juran, and Shewhart’s continual improvement.
  3. Product management phase — MIT (Richard Wang): manage information as a product, not just clean source files. GIGO misapplied: clean inputs do not guarantee a good product (El Camino example: great parts, wrong car).
  4. Enterprise architecture phase — information architecture matters. Enterprise architecture: business → information → solution → technical. Information as enterprise asset brings governance, CDO role, MDM, and analytics.
  5. Interoperability phase — sharing data across enterprises (healthcare ecosystems, supply chains). Emerging topics: ISO standards, streaming data, unstructured data, IoT, AI/LLMs, agentic data quality, data literacy, democratization, monetization.

Key figures mentioned: Larry English, Tom Redman (“the Data Doc”), Richard Wang (MIT), Danette McGilvra, Doug Laney.

Framework

Seven Principles of Information Quality

1. Data is a resource for creating value

  • Data collected, processed, and formatted for a particular use becomes an information product or service.
  • When value is created, data and information become organizational assets.
  • IQ should not be framed as merely “cleaning dirty data” — start from what customers need, then work backward to required data conditions.

2. Information is an organizational asset requiring governance

  • Data governance ≠ data management. Governance = rules for making decisions; management = the decisions themselves.
  • Serves both value maximization and risk minimization (regulation, liability, data protection).
  • Federal agencies now required to have a Chief Data Officer (CDO). CDO oversees strategy for information products, sourcing, management, and governance.

3. Information produces value when used

  • Unused data has potential value (like potential energy); value is produced only when information is used for a purpose.
  • Information product quality is measured in value units, not just conformance metrics.

4. Information should be managed as a product

  • The product of an information system — not a byproduct. CIOs focus on systems/software/hardware uptime; CDOs focus on data strategy.
  • CDO and CIO should be peer roles, not CDO under CIO.
  • Four principles of successful product management (from TQM):
    1. Understand information customer needs and wants.
    2. Manage information through well-defined, well-documented production processes.
    3. Understand product life cycle (initiation → growth → maturity → retirement).
    4. Appoint information product managers as liaisons between consumers and producers.

5. Data quality and information quality are related but distinct

  • Information quality: outward focus; measured in value (money, treatment outcomes, mission achievement); covers governance, improvement loops, and the full ecosystem.
  • Data quality: inward focus; measured as degree to which data meets requirements (ISO: quality = meeting requirements); expressed in percentages, counts, ratios.
  • Tom Redman: Is the data right? vs. Do you have the right data? Engineering analogy: building the thing right vs. building the right thing.
  • Information quality gap: internal data quality work can produce “gold-plated” products nobody wants without user feedback via product managers.

6. Information quality is contextual

  • Same data can have different information quality for different products and purposes.
  • Data quality is fixed for a given dataset and requirement set until data or requirements change.
  • Example: a name/address file with poor name quality and missing phone numbers works well for a direct-mail pizza coupon (needs mailable addresses) but poorly for an online name lookup (needs accurate names and phones).

7. Data has measurable quality dimensions

Many competing dimension frameworks exist (consultants differentiated themselves). The course primarily uses the Wang & Strong framework (MIT, research-backed): 16 dimensions in four categories:

  • Intrinsic: accuracy, believability, objectivity, reputation
  • Contextual: value-added, relevance, timeliness, completeness, appropriate amount
  • Representational: interpretability, understandability, consistency, concise representation, manipulability
  • Accessibility: accessibility and security (often in tension)

Key Quotes

Redman & GIGO

  • GIGO (garbage in, garbage out) — clean inputs do not logically guarantee good outputs.
  • Tom Redman: ask both “Is the data right?” and “Is it the right data?”
  • George Box (paraphrased): all models are wrong, but some are useful — schemas cannot anticipate every future change.

Professional Practice

IQCP Domains & Career Paths

International Association for Information and Data Quality (job analysis for Certified Information Quality Professional):

  1. Information quality value and business impact
  2. Information quality strategy and governance
  3. Information quality environment and culture
  4. Information quality measurement and improvement (sustaining IQ)
  5. Information architecture quality
  6. Data quality

Graduate career paths: data quality analysts, data governance stewards/directors, solution developers, data developers, chief data officers, data analysts, risk data analysts.

Emerging Topics

AI & Future Directions

  • LLMs are massive conditional-probability repositories — data quality matters for training data.
  • Virtuous cycle: good data builds good models; good models can help improve data (agentic data quality — covered later in the course).
  • Next frontier beyond single-enterprise IQ: interoperability, shared standards, data portability.
  • Other emerging areas: streaming/real-time DQ, unstructured data, semantic computing, IoT, automated DQ assessment/cleansing (Data Washing Machine research), automated governance.
